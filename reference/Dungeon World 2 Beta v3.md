# Introduction {#introduction}

## About This Release {#about-this-release}

This release introduces **Dungeon World 2** (DW2) to both GMs and Players. It uses the Beta rules of DW2, which are still in development. This document does not include all of the rules, classes, or similar materials from the full version of the game; those will be released in the near future at [**dungeon-world.com**](http://dungeon-world.com). Changes since the previous beta will be colored in red like this.

## What is DW2? {#what-is-dw2?}

**Dungeon World 2** (DW2) is a tabletop roleplaying game (or TTRPG) about a group of **messy people** embarking on **dangerous fantasy adventures** and growing into a **heroic found family**.

Characters embark on wondrous journeys, battle dangerous foes, take daring risks, enjoy quiet moments, and relax together as a team. They also demonstrate their talents, explore their motivations, confide in each other, and grapple with struggles past and present. Some non-TTRPG media that share this focus includes: *Baldur's Gate 3, Dungeons & Dragons: Honor Among Thieves, Guardians of the Galaxy*, and *The Mighty Nein.*

Like many TTRPGs, DW2 is played as a conversation about a shared imaginary world filled with interesting characters and events. When an uncertain or risky situation occurs, the game uses rules and mechanics to determine what happens next. Because of this, DW2 only requires a space to have the conversation (in-person or virtually), plus some dice (or a [virtual dice roller](https://rolldicewithfriends.com)), which add a random element that interacts with the rules and mechanics. It's also helpful, though not necessary, to have blank paper, writing utensils, and printouts of the rules.

The result of playing a TTRPG most closely resembles a more or less coherent story. This means that, even when the results in the fiction may not be what the participants wanted or anticipated, there are no winners or losers.

In DW2, **one** person takes on the role of **Game Master (GM**) and **everyone else** takes on the role of a single adventurer or **Player Character (PC)**. The actions, motivations, and struggles of the PCs are the central focus of what happens in the game. The GM, on the other hand, portrays the world and the rest of the characters in it, and describes the consequences of the PCs’ attempts to change the fictional milieu.

In this way, the conversation structure usually looks like this:

* The GM describes a situation in the fiction, such as, *“You* (the PCs) *are in front of a locked door.”*
* The GM finishes by asking one or more of the Players, *“What do you do?”* (or something similar)
* One or more of the Players, considering what their PC would do in such situation (AKA “roleplaying”), either says something (in 1st or 3rd person; i.e. *“I think…”* or *“My character thinks”*) and/or proposes a course of action (*“I try to pick the lock…”* or *“My character/Slick tries to pick the lock”*).
* The GM decides whether a mechanic is needed. This most often happens when the result of a proposed action is uncertain (can Slick’s lockpicking ability overcome the door’s lock?).
* When a mechanic is needed, in the case of DW2 and games like it (Powered by the Apocalypse or PbtA, in case you’re interested) that is likely a **Move**.
* In this case, the GM thinks the Player’s proposed action does fit the requirements, and immediately remembers that Slick, being a Rogue, has access to the Move **Tricks of the Trade** that includes Roguish Skills, one of which is “pick locks”.
* Considering that, the GM says to Slick’s Player, *“That sounds to me like you’re picking locks, so that’ll trigger your* **Tricks of the Trade***, right?”*
* The Player agrees, so the GM says, “*In that case, you need to roll+DEX*”.
* The Player rolls and, depending on the result, the GM incorporates the result into the conversation. For the sake of this example, let us say that Slick’s Player rolls well enough to pick the lock open.
* Now that the door is unlocked, the Players decide to open it and go forward, so now the GM needs to describe the new situation… Bringing us back to the beginning.

This may sound like a lot but, in essence, it can be summarized into five simple steps:

1. The GM describes a situation and asks the Player(s) what they do in response
2. The Player(s) propose a course of action
3. If the action has no uncertainty and/or risk, the GM updates the fiction and starts from 1\. again. If the action is uncertain/risky, the GM proposes a Move to resolve it
4. If the Player doesn’t agree with the Move proposed by the GM, they can propose an alternative or otherwise negotiate with the GM. If they do agree, the Player makes a decision and/or rolls the dice and follows the instruction on the Move
5. The GM incorporates the Move’s results to the conversation, updates the fiction, and goes back to 1\.

## The Group {#the-group}

Everyone playing this game, GM or Player, is equally deserving of dignity and a voice in the game. No one is solely responsible for the game’s “success”. Instead, the game is successful (i.e. fun) when the PCs actions and their consequences, the GM’s descriptions, and the dice rolls, all collide and combine together into a magnificent chain reaction. You are all in this together, simultaneously the co-creators and the audience of what’s happening at the table.

**In other words, every one is equally responsible for:**

* Each other’s enjoyment of the game
* Speaking up when there is an issue
* Respecting each other
* Trying to make the game experience as safe as possible for everyone involved
* Organizing and scheduling the game sessions
* Asking each other interesting questions both in- and out-of-character

## The Players {#the-players}

At the start of the first session of play, each Player creates their Player Character (PC) following the [**Character Creation**](#character-creation-cheat-sheet) procedure. During this process, players are encouraged to ask each other questions, discuss and flesh out ideas, and add interesting details to the game.

**Each Player is responsible for:**

* Collaborating with each other in- and out-of-the fiction
* Engaging with the fictional world
* Remembering the Moves they have access to
* Roleplaying their character

## The Game Master {#the-game-master}

Before play, the GM needs to familiarize themselves with the game, and come up with any ideas they’d like to include in the session. While Players create their characters, the GM leads an open discussion about the characters and the world, asking questions about the PCs, the unique Moves they have access to, their Relationships, and more. The GM is encouraged to note down the answers and use them as the basis for [**Threats**](#threats) and other adventure elements.

**The GM is responsible for:**

* Describing the world to the Players
* Having a reasonable understanding of the game’s rules, and making fair rulings when necessary
* Making [**GM Moves**](#gm-moves) that follow the game’s [**Agenda**](#agenda) and [**Principles**](#principles)
* Managing the game’s pacing and PCs’ spotlight
* Unleashing consequences

## Safety Tools {#safety-tools}

It’s fundamental to recognize that every person has their own preferences about what might be enjoyable in a game—and what might be upsetting. That’s why it’s important to talk about the game beforehand to ensure everyone is on the same page, especially regarding uncomfortable themes, topics, and similar. Furthermore, even if you talk extensively about a game beforehand, there’s always the possibility of something unexpected happening in the middle of a session that disrupts or ruins somebody’s fun.

With that in mind, the Session Zero section includes a number of Safety Tools to use to help all Participants can safely enjoy the game as much as possible.

* **The tools here only work if everyone approaches them from a kind and empathetic perspective**. They will not fix intentionally bad or uncaring behaviour.
* No one can read your mind, nor can you read others. Honestly and respectfully advocate for your own enjoyment, and make sure everyone has the chance to say their piece.
* This shouldn’t be a majority vote, and a single “no” should override several “yes”s. Work together to make space for everyone and aim to respect and consider everyone’s sensibilities.

However, even with safety tools in place sometimes there can be incompatibilities that can’t be overcome. That’s okay: it’s better to have no game experience than a bad game experience. There is no shame in leaving the game, in asking someone else to do so, or in playing something else—and/or with other people—altogether.

### Safety Tools and Related Terms {#safety-tools-and-related-terms}

**Lines and veils** (pioneered by Ron Edwards) are used to set boundaries on the allowed content in a game. **Lines are hard limits**, things that both the Players and the GM don’t want to engage with. Setting up a line means that content won’t show up in the game at all. **Veils are soft limits**, things that are okay “behind a curtain” or that, when they appear, the fiction “cuts to black”. Setting up a veil means that the content might be included in the game, but not spotlighted or described in great detail. Lines and Veils can be adjusted as needed throughout play.

**The Open Door**, designed by Eirik Fatland, is the assurance that someone can leave or take a break from the game for their own safety and well-being without being judged. State this at the beginning and periodically throughout the game session(s).

**Bleed** occurs when emotions and stress from the game and characters cause real distress in the GM or Players. This can affect the enjoyment of the game, as well as out-of-game relationships if not addressed correctly. Being open and honest about where the game begins and ends, and being conscious of which feelings are caused by real life situations and which are caused by roleplaying, can greatly help to mitigate these effects.

### X, N, and O Cards

These cards are check-in tools. They can be used by tapping physical cards, typing the letter into chat, or using hand signals. Before the game begins, remind everyone that they are available and how to use them.

The **X-Card,** created by John Stavropoulos, can be used at any point if a participant is uncomfortable with the content happening at the moment in-game. When a Player calls for the X-Card, the GM stops the game and makes sure they know what triggered it. They then add a Line (if Lines and Veils are in place) or make a note of the content to avoid. When the X-Card is used, the group can change, rewind, or skip the content.

The **N-Card** (by Mysty Vander and Adam Cleaver) can be used at any point if a participant feels like they are headed towards something that would call for an X-Card. When the N-card is used, the group can change the content or have the Scene “fade to black”.

The **O-Card**, designed by Kira Magrann, can be used at any point if someone  wants to continue with the current content and wants to check in with the rest of the table to see if they’re OK with doing so. They can also regularly be prompted by asking “O?” out loud or in chat to check-in if everyone is still okay.

### Script Change Toolbox

This is a set of cinema-themed terms, developed by Beau Jágr Sheldon, that can be used to adjust play and content as discussed.

**Rewind** can be used when something happens in play that makes a participant uncomfortable. When Rewind is used, be clear about what content is being adjusted, rewind to a point before it happened, and start playing again.

**Fast Forward** can be used when someone wants to skip past uncomfortable content or to the next part of the fiction. When it is used, skip forward to a new spot in the fiction that everyone in the game agrees on.

**Pause** can be used when things are too intense, but you still want to continue playing the Scene. When used, you can take a break and/or discuss the content as needed. After that, you can resume play, or use Rewind or Fast Forward.

### Further Tools

Visit [**ttrpgsafetytoolkit.com**](http://ttrpgsafetytoolkit.com) for more tools beyond what is present here.

# Playing the Game {#playing-the-game}

## The Conversation {#the-conversation}

At its core, **Dungeon World 2** is a group conversation in a shared imaginative space, often called "the fiction" or "the narrative". Everybody takes turns describing what happens in the narrative, with Players presenting what their characters do, say, and feel as their characters (PCs), and the GM describing how the world reacts to the PCs’ actions.

Generally, the cycle of the ongoing conversation looks like this:

1. **The GM presents a dramatic situation for the PCs to respond to**, often making a Move that follows their **[Agenda](#agenda)** and [**Principles**](#principles). This may be a tempting opportunity, intriguing clue, dangerous attack, jovial banter, ominous vision, tragic revelation, and/or much more. Often, the GM will end their description by asking *"What do you do?"* If multiple PCs are involved, the GM may ask them as a group or may focus on a specific PC.

2. **The Player(s) describe their response**. This may involve a bit of back-and-forth with the GM to clarify some details and what is reasonable to accomplish. Breaking through a barred door with your fists and feet might not be feasible, but if you use a huge axe it may very well be.

3. **The GM decides if a Move is triggered**. Most Moves begin with a narrative hook in bolded text that "triggers" when met (e.g. “When you engage a foe in battle…”).  When this trigger happens the Move usually proceeds as-written, accounting for [**Fictional Positioning**](#fictional-positioning).

4. **If a Move is triggered** the Player resolves it, usually rolling dice as part of that. Some Moves, like **Aid a PC**, are meant to boost other Moves, so Players can describe how they trigger them to help another Move. Once a Move is resolved, it changes the fiction going forward.

5. **The GM describes how things change**, often building upon this change to present the next dramatic situation by returning to Step 1\.

## Play to Find Out {#play-to-find-out}

The most important, foundational idea that underlies all of **Dungeon World 2**’s design is a very simple one: no one, Player or GM, should know with certainty what will happen next in the game. In short: **play to find out**.

This means that everyone trusts each other to discover and lead the action from moment-to-moment, rather than rigidly plan for particular outcomes. By following the [**Player’s Best Practices**](#player’s-best-practices), GM [**Agenda**](#agenda) and [**Principles**](#principles), as well as the rest of the Moves and procedures presented here, everyone works together to let their actions—and their consequences—guide the conversation forward towards interesting and dramatic situations.

That’s why the adventures we offer you (and the procedures to create them) aren’t structured around specific outcomes or the PCs acting in a certain manner. In contrast, the GM is offered complicated situations and distinct elements to present to the Players, and the PCs actions will determine the possible ways in which things will evolve.

## Player’s Best Practices {#player’s-best-practices}

Each Player is free to play their PC however they want to, but if you’d like to get the best out of the experience, we recommend that you follow these principles.

### Accept Losses Gracefully {#accept-losses-gracefully}

When your PC’s sword breaks in a fight, they can still swing with their fists. When the PCs fail to save the prince’s life, you can roleplay chasing down the assassin. And when someone’s beloved fictional village lies in ruin, your PC can pick up their hammer and help with the rebuilding.

A clean success in DW2 is statistically rare so you'll most often get dice results that imply success mixed with failure and/or a cost. But even total failure is not the end, because you can always deal with the consequences and progress in the fiction.

If your PC loses something—be it an NPC relation, an important possession, or an opportunity they fought hard for—then you have the chance to roleplay how your character deals with loss.

### Cooperate with Each Other

Everyone at the table should work together to have the most fun possible. Even when your PCs are separated or arguing with each other, you as Players are on the same team. As such, here are some of our top recommendations for great cooperation at the table:

* Before starting or continuing an extended Scene on your own, such as the Rogue sneaking off to steal something, **check in with the others to see** if they’re alright with that, or **if they want the spotlight** for a bit.
* Before significantly harming or altering another PC—or something important to them—**check to see if their Player consents to such harm and/or alteration**.
* **Try to make sure everyone gets a chance to speak**. Group dynamics are hard to navigate, and some people can unintentionally be louder or quieter than others. We advise that you listen intently and don’t interrupt when someone else is speaking, and to check with anyone who hasn’t spoken up in a while.
* Treat each other with respect, and uphold boundaries, especially those established through **Safety Tools**. For example, if the Cleric’s uncle character passing away tragically would make the game less fun (for anyone in the game) then work together and search for another consequence.

### Engage With the Fictional World

Don't play your PC as a “lone wolf”. Connections and related NPCs are interesting and can often serve as compelling motivations for adventure. Anchor your character's past to particular places, people, and/or events. Also, don’t forget to build relationships with other PCs; admiration, competition, protectiveness, mentorship, family, romance, ideological conflict, and loyalty are just some aspects of relationships you can explore.

### Explore the Player Characters

Roleplay your character as best as you can. This includes expressing their thoughts, feelings, and motivation(s), either through first person “acting” (e.g. looking sad when your PC feels sad) or third person descriptions (e.g. my character/Duskmantle feels sad…) . We recommend that you start with a relatively simple character concept, then develop nuances and/or reveal details over time.

Finally, we advise that you ask each other questions, in- and out-of-character, and be generous with your responses when asked questions in return.

### Play Dangerously

Safety and patience are for real life (and most NPCs in the fiction), but they rarely make for fun adventures. Your PC shines the brightest when they’re at their limit. As such, we recommend that you roleplay decisively and in the moment, pursuing dangerous fictional objectives/goals and acting on the opportunities that arise in the game. We advise you keep complex plans and preparations offscreen or in flashbacks, as they often abruptly stop the narrative flow.

We think that PCs should be easy to bruise but hard to break. According to the rules, even when in mortal danger, your character will only die when you risk it or choose to (see [**Face Death**](#face-death)). That means that you make dangerous and exciting decisions for your character because their ultimate fate is always in your hands.

## Scenes {#scenes}

**Dungeon World 2** is a game structured around **Scenes**: situations of high tension, dangerous conflict, and/or crucial uncertainty in the continuous, collaboratively-built narrative we refer to in shorthand as “the fiction”. The fiction emerges naturally as a result of simple moments of conversation and roleplay.

The GM is usually the one setting up most Scenes, and they are the ultimate arbiter on whether a Scene gets played or not, but the Players are encouraged to propose Scenes they would like to play. This is especially useful when the PCs **Make Camp**, **Enjoy Downtime**, or are having other moments of roleplay between them. Moreover, everyone in the game is responsible for roleplay in the Scenes following what makes the most sense in the fiction as established so far (see Fictional Positioning below).

The most important aspect of a Scene is to have an uncertainty that needs resolving. It may be a mystery the GM presented and the PCs want to decipher, or a tense Scene where a PC-to-PC Relationship can be established or modified, among many others.

There are many techniques and approaches on how to accomplish this (we share some with the GM later), but here’s a simple one that’s good to keep in mind: **skip the boring stuff**. What’s “boring” will depend highly on what everyone considers fun (and especially not fun), so it may take some time for a new group—or even an old group starting a new game—to find the right balance. In case of doubt, we recommend you focus on what really interests you about the game… And avoid most everything else.

And speaking of avoiding things…

### In Medias Res

Sometimes the GM will start a Scene *in medias res*; i.e. in the middle of the action. The PCs will be forced to immediately react and do things without much context or preparation, and the Players may feel a bit confused and uncomfortable.

If you’re playing a PC, we recommend you resist the urge to stop and plan, or to renegotiate the Conditions of the Scene. Instead, think about what your character would do and, following one of the **Player’s Best Practices** described above ([“Accept Losses Gracefully”](#accept-losses-gracefully)), just *roll with it* and see where it gets you. We believe everyone in the game will have a much better time playing this way than going for a more cautious, undramatic approach.

### “*What Do You Do?*”

The most important question that’ll guide the conversation—and the fiction—is “*What do you do?”* The GM will most often ask the question to the PCs as a group (after presenting them with a given situation) and, at other times, they’ll prompt a specific PC.

*“What do you do?”* is an invitation for Players to add to the conversation , to roleplay their characters in the moment, and act in a way that pushes the conversation forward. In this sense, it’s good to remember that action is almost always more dramatic than planning or discussing what to do. The former will most probably advance and/or complicate the fictional situation, while the latter can lead to slowing down or even stopping all dramatic momentum. Remember that another way to express **Dungeon World 2**’s vision is to say that it’s **a game about interesting fantasy people acting decisively under great pressure**. So, *don’t feel afraid to increase the pressure*.

## Fictional Positioning {#fictional-positioning}

The result of the interaction between conversation and game mechanics is **the fiction**. That fiction starts building before any dice are rolled during [**Session Zero**](#session-zero), with the Players and GM establishing boundaries and agreeing on certain themes and/or ideas they want to explore. During this session (or even before it) the GM may present the Players with some ideas about the fantasy world they’d like to explore, either inspired by a known fantasy world or one of their own creation. The most important thing to remember here is that, in either case, DW2 is about **playing to find out**, so no detail or preference should make it so a specific outcome in the fiction is predetermined.

Once gameplay starts, all characters in the fiction have certain abilities (often determined by their Class) or carry possessions (any and all Equipment) that make it so certain actions should be reasonably easier or harder for them to pull off. For example, a diminutive pixie can’t reasonably swing a greataxe—unless there’s an explanation like magic or something else. Those properties and “reasonable”/logical things in the fiction we refer to in general as **fictional positioning**.

Fictional positioning is a responsibility of everyone in the game; that’s why an important part of the conversation is about clarifying what’s currently happening in the fiction and especially how the PCs are situated in relation to it. Moves interact directly with fictional positioning, as the decision to trigger them—or not—by the GM is often highly dependent on it. Coming back to the example of the diminutive pixie, it’d be well within reason for a GM to declare that the PC cannot **Trade Blows** with a greataxe they cannot wield\!

## Session Zero {#session-zero}

Before you start rolling the dice and facing dangers, we recommend that you discuss the game at length to get everyone on the same page about what kind of game this will be. This might be a group chat in the weeks leading up to the game, or an in-person sit-down for the first hour or two of the first session.

Be honest with and kind to each other, listen respectfully, and try to get everyone to contribute. Work together to create something that you’re all interested in, and pay special attention if someone says no. This discussion is not restricted to the start of the game; so feel free to revisit it in the future.

### Talk About the Game

Example topics to discuss include:

* *What type(s) of fantasy are you interested i*n (dark, epic, grim, steampunk, sword & sorcery, etc.)?
* *What ideas do you have for the game?*
* *What types of adventures would you like your PC(s) to have?*
* *What story elements and/or themes would you like to explore?*
* *How long would you like the game to last* (one-shot, short adventure, campaign, long campaign, etc.)*?*
* *What things you'd like to be off-limits* (see “Lines and Veils” under [**Safety Tools**](#safety-tools-and-related-terms))*?*
* *What do and don’t you want to see in the game?*
* *What* [**Safety Tools**](#safety-tools) *will you use?*
* *What kind of character would you want to play?*

### Talk About the Fantasy World

We advise that you try and establish as clear a vision for the fantasy world you're adventuring in as possible. Perhaps it's a custom world of the GM's creation, or a world from a fantasy book or movie, or a world you co-create together here and now.

As tempting as it can be (especially as the GM), we don’t recommend establishing everything about the world right now. Instead, we think that focusing on what you need to start adventuring, and add in anything else you find interesting as you play, will serve you much better. We feel that leaving plenty of room for compelling mysteries, unanswered questions, and empty spaces that can be later filled in will probably lead to more fun in the long run.

Here are some example questions to discuss about the area in which the adventure starts. *The last question* of each is intended to create opportunities for adventure.

* What is the nearby landscape like? The weather? *What strange phenomenon is unique to this area?*
* What ancestries coexist here? How peaceful is that coexistence? How common are humans in the world? *Which ancestry recently appeared in the world, or vanished from it?*
* What gods are revered or worshipped? Are they plainly real, or is their existence a matter of faith? *What rumors or secrets follow them or their followers?*
* Who rules or governs this land? How have they maintained power or authority? *Who works to undermine them?*
* What cultural arts or practices are unique to this area? How are stories, traditions, and lessons remembered and maintained? *Who's trying to twist or silence them?*
* How accepted is magic in this area? Is it seen as a convenient tool, mysterious wonder, or unstable danger? How common is its practice or study? *What magical curse or catastrophe still shapes this area?*
* How technologically advanced is this area? What unique technology or tools have been developed? *What resource or risk of their creation is becoming less sustainable?*
* When did war last sweep this area? What was forever lost in the bloodshed? What finally brought peace? *What encroaching threat or simmering conflict might bring war again?*
* How common are adventurers, and how are they treated? Are they valued heroes or taken for granted? How often do people rely on them instead of guards or soldiers? *What infamous adventurer(s) give them a bad name?*

### Talk About the Player Characters

After each Player goes through [**Character Creation**](#character-creation), you can **Introduce Your PC**, going around the table so everyone gets the chance to talk about their PC a bit. During this section all—Players and the GM alike— are invited to ask questions, draw out details, and establish how the PCs got together as a group.

Encourage each other to share as much or as little detail as you want about your characters. Also remember that you can always say “*You as Players would know this, but your characters wouldn’t*” or something similar, so you can separate things known in the Conversation (Players) vs things known in the fiction (PCs).

* Share your name, pronouns, and appearance. *What memorable features or behaviors do you have?*
* *What are your Origins?* Express some ideas about your [**Ancestry**](#ancestry), [**Community**](#community), and [**Background**](#background), such as by answering the questions listed under each in your Class.
* Establish a Relationship with one other PC (hopefully connecting the whole group in this way), using the examples on each Class or writing your own. *How did it come to be?* (For multi-session adventures or campaigns, you can create one Relationship with each other PC now or add them when you [**Comfort or Support**](#comfort-or-support) someone).
* *How did you all come together as a group?* Because of your Relationships you didn't just meet, but by default you're all still fairly new to adventuring together.

### Take a Break

You've probably spent a while doing some—or all\!—of the above, and the GM may need a bit of time to turn their notes into something playable right now (if you’re playing a session right after this)..

### Start Playing

When you feel ready to begin, the GM will set the first Scene of the game.

## Crash Course in (Understanding & Using) Moves {#crash-course-in-(understanding-&-using)-moves}

A Move is a **self-contained rule that defines what happens when a specific action is taken in the fiction**. When a PC does something risky, dangerous, or tense, and the group needs to determine how it resolves, it often results in a Move triggering. As such, we recommend you familiarize yourselves with the Moves available, especially the [**Core Moves**](#core-moves).

### Triggering Moves

Many Moves start with “When you **do X**” to describe when they trigger (e.g. “When you **scrutinize someone throughout a conversation**…”), while others change or modify an existing Move (e.g. “When you [**Spout Lore**](#spout-lore-\(+questions\)), you may ignore the questions…”).

If a Move makes sense for the current situation, follow its text to resolve it. Sometimes Moves will say to roll dice plus a Stat, answer questions, or just make choices when following them. Once the Move is resolved, resume the conversation, incorporating any changes the Move made in the fiction.

Some Moves say “Once per session…” at the start. This means that the Move only happens the first time its trigger is fulfilled in each session. Most of these are limited on an individual basis so, If there are three PCs in the group, for example, each can trigger such a Move once.

Some Moves are available to all PCs; namely, the [**Core**](#core-moves), [**Extra**](#extra-moves), and [**Campaign**](#campaign-moves) Moves. Other Moves are only available by choosing a certain Class, or a Path within a Class.

There is also a list of GM Moves, split up into [**Core**](#gm-core-moves) and [**Class**](#gm-class-moves) Moves, which work very differently from Player-facing Moves. Instead of triggering as a result of a specific action happening in the fiction, all GM Moves happen when the GM is compelled to add to the conversation following a set of guidelines divided into an [**Agenda**](#agenda) and [**Principles**](#principles) that best fit the spirit of a **Dungeon World 2** game.

It's usually fairly obvious, but when this game says "Move" and references a PC's actions and potential outcomes, it likely means a PC Move. If it references the GM's descriptions and reactions to a PC action, it probably means a GM Move.

### Rolling Dice

Many Moves call for dice to be rolled to resolve them. When you roll, you do so with two six-sided dice (often referred to as “2d6”, or "dice" by non-TTRPG enthusiasts), and add the numbers together. You usually also add a Stat to the roll; so when a Move says “roll+STR” that means you roll 2d6 and add their numbers together—adding your Strength stat (which is shortened to STR)—for a total. For example: *if you rolled a 4 and a 5, and your STR is \-1, then your final result would be 8*.

The only exception to this is when rolling for damage (or dmg), which is explained below.

Instead of rolling with a Stat, some Moves instead ask you questions about the narrative and change your roll depending on the answers, adding \+1 for each “Yes”.

### Move Results

* Rolling **a total of 10 or higher (✷10+)** means **you get what you want**, or as close to it as feasible, while avoiding most of the consequences. This doesn't always mean a complete success—depending on the Move and choices—but it's still something to rejoice.
* Rolling **a total of 7, 8, or 9 (✷7-9)** means **you achieve what you want, but there’s a cost, consequence, or the effect is reduced** or different from what was intended.
* Rolling **a total of 6 or lower (✷6-)** means **things don't go well, and you don't quite get what you want**, or you get it if you pay an extreme cost or suffer a terrible consequence. In such cases often the GM will decide what happens.

**The GM only rolls damage dice for the characters they portray** (NPCs). If the PCs act to resolve a situation that involves, affects, or opposes an NPC, then it’s the PC’s Moves and rolls that will inform the success of the NPC's actions.

So, for example, if a PC [**Trades Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\)) with an NPC, depending on the result of the roll and the Player’s decisions, the NPC may inflict the dmg the GM rolled, max, or min, or the PC will have to mark a Condition, the NPC gets a fleeting opportunity, or the PC loses or breaks something.

In the case when a PC interacts with an NPC in a non-aggressive way, the NPC’s actions are determined by the parameters of the Move the PC triggers, such as information revealed by [**Sense Motive**](#sense-motive-\(+wis\)) questions, or getting an NPC to act a certain way thanks to successfully [**Pulling**](#pull-strings-\(+cha\)) their **Strings**.

### Advantage and Disadvantage

Sometimes a PC might have Advantage (adv) or Disadvantage (dis) on a roll.

* When you have adv, roll three dice (3d6) and **ignore the lowest result, keeping the highest two**.
* When you have dis, roll three dice (3d6) and **ignore the highest result, keeping the lowest two**.

For example, if you had adv on a roll and the dice came up as 2, 4, and 6, then you'd ignore the 2 and keep the 4 and 6, totalling 10\. If you had dis on the same roll then you'd instead ignore the 6 and keep the 2 and 4, totalling 6\.

**adv and dis cancel each other out**. For example, if you have two sources of adv and one of dis, you roll with adv.

You can never have more than 1 adv or dis total, so you never roll more than three dice (3d6).

### Misses vs Failures

A roll of 6- is often called a miss and not a failure… and that’s intentional. Although most of the time a PC rolls a 6- they won’t get what they want—and thus, it’ll feel like a failure—that isn’t always the case.

For example, if a PC is trying to pick the lock of a door, a 6- may be interpreted by the GM as the PC successfully picking the lock… But finding 10 guards waiting for them on the other side.

A GM may choose to do this for a variety of reasons, but the important thing here is to keep in mind that a 6- should always propel the conversation forward, adding new elements to it, instead of bringing it to a stop.

# Character Creation {#character-creation}

## Character Creation Cheat Sheet {#character-creation-cheat-sheet}

1. **Choose a [Class](#the-classes)**. The foundation of a PC is their Class, the fantasy archetype they embody and express. A PC's Class influences their specialties and fortes. There can be only one of each Class at the table at the same time so, if multiple people want to play the same Class, work with each other to find a happy compromise.
2. **Consider Your Origins.** These are three aspects of your character that have no specific mechanics associated with them, but that establish important [**fictional positioning**](#fictional-positioning) about them. They are your character’s [**Ancestry**](#ancestry), [**Community**](#community), and [**Background**](#background).
3. **Familiarize Yourself with Your Class Starting Move.** Read and try to understand it to the best of your ability, asking the GM any questions you may have. Some Starting Moves may ask you to make a choice.
4. **Choose Equipment and Appearance.** Each Class lists a number of items, such as weapons, armor, or supplies for you to choose between, plus some cosmetic choices about your appearance. Make your choices and mark them on your character sheet.
5. **Assign Stats.** There are five Stats in **Dungeon World 2**:
   1. **Strength** (STR) represents how good you are fighting, pushing others around, and enduring harm
   2. **Dexterity** (DEX) represents how good you are at disappearing, moving quickly, aiming precisely, and escaping notice
   3. **Intelligence** (INT) represents how much you can notice, study, deduce, and calculate
   4. **Wisdom** (WIS) represents your ability to empathize with people and intuitively understand the supernatural
   5. **Charisma** (CHA) represents your ability to sway people, get their attention, and make a good impression

You start with a \+2, \+1, 0, 0, and \-1. Your Class will guide you on how to assign them.

1. **Write Your Character’s Name and Pronouns.** Each Class lists some example names.
2. **Introduce Your PC (**and Talk About the Characters). Briefly describe your character, using their name, pronouns, and Class, as well as incorporating details such as their Origins and Equipment/Appearance choices.
3. **Establish a Relationship with Another PC.** Every PC has a Relationship with another PC, such as a belief, event, goal, or feeling related to them. Each Class lists some examples. The Depth of a new Relationship starts at 0, but increase it by 1 if you’ve adventured together before, and increase it by another 1 if you grew up together. (For multi-session adventures or campaigns, you can create one Relationship with each other PC now or add them when you [**Comfort or Support**](#comfort-or-support) someone).

## Class {#class}

The Classes available in DW2 are: [**The Barbarian**](#the-barbarian), [**The Bard**](#the-bard), [**The Cleric**](#the-cleric), [**The Druid**](#the-druid), [**The Fighter**](#the-fighter), [**The Paladin**](#the-paladin), [**The Ranger**](#the-ranger), [**The Rogue**](#the-rogue), and [**The Wizard**](#the-wizard).

### Changing Classes

When your character has changed significantly due to choice, consequence, or circumstance, it may be time to change their Class. Changing a character's Class typically represents a new chapter in their life. They may not have forgotten the past and how they used to do things, but they’ve left a significant part of that behind. You can change Classes when you [**Level Up**](#level-up).

1. **Choose your new Class.** *For example, Erinyette the level 5 Rogue resolved her* [**Reformed**](#reformed-—-guilt-🗹🗹☐) *Struggle, and decided to change to the Paladin Class to represent her determination to fight for what’s right.*
2. **Assign your Stats as if you were creating a new character.** You can use your old Stats as guidance or completely ignore them and use this opportunity to start with a clean slate.
3. **Gain your new Class Starting Move**. Lose the one from your old Class.
4. **Reassign your Advancements.** Count the number of advancements you had from your old Class, and gain the same number in your old Class.
   *Erinyette had 4 Advancements as a Rogue. Her Player decides to keep the* **Burglar** *Path and it’s Starting Move,* [**Expert Treasure-hunter**](#expert-treasure-hunter) *as a Multiclass, but she’ll use the other 3 Advancements to improve her WIS, unlock the* **Inquisitor** *Path (their nemesis has hidden devotees everywhere), and to reveal a new Struggle:* [**Vengeful**](#vengeful)*.*

5. **Your XP, Level, and Treasure Track remain unchanged.**
6. **Your max HP changes to that of your new Class**, modified by your per Level bonus (+1 per Level beyond 1, max \+10). Your current HP goes up or down by the same amount (this can't lower it below 1).

## Origins {#origins}

The three aspects that make up your Origins are:

### Ancestry {#ancestry}

This is usually who your parents were or who created you. Classic options in fantasy worlds include *dwarf, elf, human*, and *halfling*. More modern popular choices include *construct*, *dragonborn*, and *tiefling*. These days, Players seem to love *catfolk*, *fungiperson*, and *goblin*, just to name a few.

### Community {#community}

This is the environment you were raised in, which can or cannot be the same as that of your parents/creator(s). Popular options in fantasy worlds include *a mercenary company, road scholars, scheming nobles*, and *a tightly-knit tribe*, among many others.

### Background {#background}

This is your character’s former occupation or profession before you started adventuring. Popular options in fantasy worlds include *acolyte, criminal, mage’s apprentice*, and *soldier*, among many others.

## Stats {#stats}

Each PC has five Stats which affect not just what they can do, but also how they might behave or how others perceive them.

* **Strength** (STR) represents **physical power** and **endurance**. It’s the key Stat to [**Trade Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\)) in **hand-to-hand combat** and it’s a requirement to know how to use armor properly.
* **Dexterity** (DEX) represents **speed, reflexes,** and **agility**. It’s the key Stat to [**Trade Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\)) in **ranged combat** and it’s the key Stat to [**Sneak Past**](#sneak-past-\(+dex\)).
* **Intelligence** (INT) represents **perception** and **calculation**. It’s the key Stat to [**Unearth Secrets**](#unearth-secrets-\(+int\)). It is NOT used to remember things, as any PC might know or recall different information based on their past experiences.
* **Wisdom** (WIS) represents **empathy, intuition**, and **instinct**. It’s the key Stat to [**Sense Motive**](#sense-motive-\(+wis\)).
* **Charisma** (CHA) represents **allure, authority**, and **cordiality**. It’s the key Stat to [**Pull Strings**](#pull-strings-\(+cha\)).

## Conditions {#conditions}

Conditions represent exhaustion, frustration, maladies, or emotions that affect a PC as they adventure. They usually happen as a result of a Player’s choices, and are rarely inflicted specifically upon you. A Condition can be either **marked** or **locked**. There are five total Conditions:

* **Angry**. When marked or locked you have dis on all \+INT rolls. *Who or what are you angry at? Does it simmer or explode?*
* **Distracted**. When marked or locked you have dis on all \+WIS rolls. *What stubborn worry or feeling won’t go away? How does it manifest?*
* **Exhausted**. When marked or locked you have dis on all \+STR rolls. *What tired you out? How does your fatigue show?*
* **Frightened**. When marked or locked you have dis on all \+DEX rolls. *What fear or memory is related? Why do your instincts scream?*
* **Insecure**. When marked or locked you have dis on all \+CHA rolls. *What made you feel you might not be enough? How do you react?*

### Clear, Marked, and Locked Conditions

Each Condition is represented by a circle on a PC’s character sheet, which has three states:
◯ **Clear** — The circle representing the Condition is empty. The Condition is inactive and it doesn’t affect your character.

**⦵ Marked** — The circle has a line or two through it, representing that the Condition is active and it’s currently affecting your character.

**⬤ Locked** — The circle is fully filled in. Not only is the Condition active and affecting your character, but it won’t clear under normal circumstances. Only effects that specify that they clear locked Conditions can do so, such as [**Enjoy Downtime**](#enjoy-downtime) or [**Reveal Your Struggle**](#reveal-your-struggle).

When a PC has to mark or lock a Condition, their Player chooses one and then describes how their character suffers it in the fiction. While they have a marked or locked Condition, the PC can use the questions beside it to inform how it might affect their roleplaying.

Rare circumstances, such using certain *magic items*, may require a PC to mark a specific Condition. If  they already have that Condition marked, they instead lock it. If it's already locked, then they mark another Condition of their choice.

When a PC marks their last Condition, they trigger [**Reach Your Limit**](#reach-your-limit).

## Starting Move {#starting-move}

This Move is core to the Class and can only be used by a PC who is of that Class. For example, anyone can go on stage and show off their entertainment skills, but only The Bard can trigger [**Perform Your Art**](#perform-your-art).

## Hit Points & Damage {#hit-points-&-damage}

### Hit Points

Hit Points (HP) are **an abstraction that represents a character’s physical well-being**. PCs and NPCs have variable amounts of it. In the case of PCs, that amount is mostly determined by their Class and Level.

When a character suffers damage—mostly as a result of [**Trading Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\)) or other dangers of adventuring—they decrease their current HP by a specific amount, usually determined by the GM rolling appropriate dice. This might represent small scratches, significant wounds, loss of willpower, or general fatigue.

* When any character, PC or NPC, has less than half their maximum HP, they’re **Bloodied**. While Bloodied, your injuries are obvious to all, and characters will react accordingly. Some Moves or other mechanics might reference being Bloodied.
* When a PC has lost all their HP, they’re **Helpless**. If they want to act significantly in the fiction, they first need to [**Face Death**](#face-death)**.** Most NPCs who lose their HP are either defeated or die of their wounds.

### Damage

A weapon or attack's damage (dmg) is shown like this: **1d8 dmg**. When dmg is dealt, the character inflicting it rolls the die or dice (one eight-sided die in the example above) to determine it.

Similar to Moves, dmg can also have adv and dis. This means that you roll one additional die with the same number of faces and use the highest (adv) or lowest (dis) result. For example, if your weapon deals 1d8 dmg and you deal dmg with adv, you would roll two eight-sided dice and keep the highest one.

Some effects, notably [**Trade Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\)), say that minimum (min) or maximum (max) dmg is dealt. In those situations, instead of rolling, treat the roll as it was the lowest or highest possible. This *does still count as rolling for damage* when it comes to triggering certain Moves and effects, even if dice weren't physically (or digitally) rolled.

**Dual Wielding:** When dealing dmg with two weapons at once, roll dmg for each and keep the highest result. By default, only \#light weapons are designed to be wielded this way.

#### Damage Dice Size {#damage-dice-size}

Some effects can increase or decrease the dmg die size. **Dungeon World 2**'s polyhedral die size levels are as follows:

| d4 | d6 | d8 | d10 | d12 | d12+d4 | d12+d6 | d12+d8 | d12+d10 |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |

Most damage falls within between 1d4 and 1d10, but some can get much larger.

#### Resistance

When a PC or NPC has preparations for, defenses against, or immunity to an effect, approach, or source of damage, they **Resist** it, meaning it's nearly useless against them. Resistance does not provide dmg reduction like armor does, but instead influences the [Fictional Positioning](#fictional-positioning) and whether dmg (and Moves) would be rolled *at all*. Overcoming such resistance usually requires imagination, special circumstances, or overwhelming power.

When an NPC Resists a PC's efforts, the GM has a golden opportunity to make a Move. Similarly, when a PC Resists something an NPC does, the GM should usually give that PC a similar opportunity.

## Equipment & \#tags {#equipment-&-#tags}

A PCs belongings can sometimes make the difference between life and death. Each PC chooses equipment from lists based on their Class. Don't worry about carrying capacity or inventory load—you can carry as much as it makes sense in the fiction.

### Uses

Some items have one or more circles ◯ next them, indicating they have a limited number of uses. Each time you mark a use, cross one of the circles. When the last circle is marked, you can't use the item anymore—it may be depleted, spoiled, broken, or similar.

### Tags

Some items have tags, which are all listed after the name alongside the \#symbol. For example, a sword might have the tags \#hand and \#versatile. These tags signal the item's advantages and limitations.

Here is the list of all tags in the game.

\#ammo — it needs ammunition to work. By default you always have enough, but you might run out as a result of a GM Move
\#area — can affect multiple targets in close proximity
\#close — the target must be up to a few paces away, close enough to see their face expressions but not to touch
\#concealed — easy to hide from others unless they search you thoroughly
\#distinctive — has an obvious sound or appearance that attracts attention
\#far — you must be able to clearly see your target, or to hear them if they yell at the top of their lungs.
\#forceful — inflicts powerful blows that knock targets back and down
\#hand — the target must be within grabbing distance
\#holy — blessed by a divine power, and thus especially effective against a deity’s enemies; adv on dmg rolls against them
\#light — easily dual-wielded with similar weapons (roll dmg for both and keep highest)
\#near — the target must be up to speaking distance, even if you may have to raise your voice
\#piercing — cuts or bypasses thick armor and shielding. dmg of this type ignores [armor and shield protection](#armor-&-shields)
\#precise — requires quick and careful aim to use well; [**Trade Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\))\+DEX (instead of \+STR).
\#reload — after you use it, you need to spend time reloading, rearming, or recharging it to use it again
\#thrown — you can [**Trade Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\))\+STR instead of \+DEX when using it as a ranged weapon
\#two-handed — needs two hands to use properly, maybe for weight, balance, a second part of it, etc.

### Armor & Shields {#armor-&-shields}

Worn armor decreases damage taken by its wearer. Every time you receive damage from an enemy, the environment, or similar, decrease it by your Armor. **Armor cannot decrease damage below 1\.** There will always be at least a few cuts and bruises from an attack, even if your armor stopped most of it.

Armor also encumbers its wearer if they aren't strong enough to manage its bulk. If your STR is below the armor's requirement (usually equal to its armor value) then you don’t receive its protective benefit.

#### Armor Types

* **Light armor**, such as sturdy hides and padded gambesons, has dmg reduction 1 (reduces all incoming dmg by 1, to a minimum of 1\) and it requires a minimum STR+1 to use properly
* **Medium armor**, like a chain shirt, breastplate, or scale mail, has dmg reduction 2 (reduces all incoming dmg by 2, to a minimum of 1\) and it requires a minimum STR+2 to use properly
* **Heavy armor**, like a proper chain mail or full plate, has dmg reduction 3 (reduces all incoming dmg by 3, to a minimum of 1\) and it requires a minimum STR+3 to use properly

#### Shields

Shields work differently from armor. Rather than decreasing dmg a little each time you suffer it, you can use your shield once to ignore all damage from a single attack. **Unlike armor, shields can reduce damage taken to zero**. You can use a shield this way after damage is rolled, so you know how useful it'll be in advance. When you use your shield, mark the circle near it to show it as used. When you finish [**Making Camp**](#make-camp), you can erase the mark and regain the use of your shield.

## XP, Level, and Advancements {#xp,-level,-and-advancements}

### Experience (XP)

Over time the PCs become stronger, represented by their accrued **Experience (XP)**. XP is tracked separately for each PC. It starts at 0, and PCs mark it any time a Move says "mark 1 XP", most often when a 6- is rolled or when answering [**End the Session**](#end-the-session) questions.

### Level

**Level** represents the overall advancement of a PC, and starts at 1\.

### Leveling Up & Advancements

When a Player marks the 5th XP on their PC’s Experience Track, the character [**Levels Up**](#level-up), resetting XP to 0\. increasing their Level by one, adding \+1 to their current and maximum HP (up to \+10 in total), and gaining a chosen benefit—an Advancement—from the specific list associated with their Class. This can happen in the middle of battle or another climactic moment.

If [**Leveling Up**](#level-up) would distract from the action and narrative, rather than add to it, the Player may choose to wait until a more relaxed moment—such as while [**Making Camp**](#make-camp)—to [**Level Up**](#level-up).

### Paths & Advanced Moves

Each Class features three Paths, which contain Moves and similar abilities a PC can unlock over time.

* When you **gain a Path**, choose a Path from your Class that you don't have yet (or from another Class if it's a Multiclass Path) and mark it to show that you've gained it. You also gain its Starting Move—the first one listed within it.
* When you **gain a Path Move**, choose a Move you don't have (within a Path you *do* have), then mark and gain it.

With enough time and growth, a PC can eventually have two Paths from their own Class and two Paths from other Classes. When taking a Path that another PC already has or might have, such as from their Class, ask them for permission first. Two PCs can both have the same Path, but take care to not overshadow each other.

# The Moves {#the-moves}

## Core Moves {#core-moves}

These Moves are supposed to be triggered frequently, and form the backbone of the DW2 experience.

### Aid a PC (N/A) {#aid-a-pc-(n/a)}

| While you help another PC, they have adv on their rolls. Before the helped PC rolls, you must choose one: *It's risky*; all consequences from this also affect you *It's stressful*; mark a Condition *They frustrate you*; erase 1 Depth with them |
| :---- |

*“All consequences from this also affect you”* means those consequences are applied in full to both the aided and aiding PC, rather than being divided between them. For example, if the aided PC suffers damage, the aiding PC suffers the same amount. If one is caught and captured, the other one is too.

### Cast a Spell (+Variable) {#cast-a-spell-(+variable)}

| When you invoke magic you can use, say what you want to happen; the GM will tell you the effect's mag and then you compare it to your Casting Stat (see the Class or Path that is your magic’s source).  If the mag *exceeds* your Casting Stat, the spell is beyond you; negotiate a lesser effect or [Perform a Ritual](#perform-a-ritual) to achieve it If the mag *equals* your Casting Stat, roll+Casting Stat with dis If the mag is *lesser than* your Casting Stat, roll+Casting Stat ✷On a 10+, the effect comes to fruition as intended. ✷On a 7-9, it happens, but choose one: A unique option depending on your magic’s source The effort is tiring; mark a Condition There’s a twist; the GM will add an “and” or “but” to its effect ✷On a 6-, something goes terribly wrong; mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. |
| :---- |

“*Magic you can use*” is most usually granted by a Class Starting Move or Path Move, but some *magic items* or *Artifacts* might let you **Cast a Spell**. These and other options will always clearly state that you can **Cast a Spell** in some way.

#### Magnitude (mag)

Magnitude (mag) represents **a spell's overall power**. The GM has the final say on a spell's mag, based on the effect's severity, target, range, duration, area, and similar factors. These guidelines below should help:

* **mag 0** — Minor magic (sometimes called cantrips, orisons, or rote spells) can be executed even by beginners. They are the equivalent of 5e's Spell Levels 0 and 1\. These could be achieved without magic at all. Examples include: *shoot a magical dart (*1d4 dmg)*; heal scratches and bruises* (1d4 HP restored)*, communicate from across a room; summon a tiny sprite; read someone's current emotions; charm someone distracted or exhausted; induce a light fitful, sleep; slow down a fall; turn someone invisible for a few seconds, repair ordinary wear and tear.*
* **mag 1** — Standard magic commonly done by ordinary practitioners, the equivalent of 5e's Spell Levels 2 and 3\. Doing something similar without magic would require complex, lengthy, or coordinated effort. Examples include: *shoot an acid arrow (*1d8 dmg \#piercing*); heal deep cuts* (1d8 HP restored)*; communicate from across a country; summon a sly imp; read someone’s surface thoughts; charm an ordinary person; induce a deep but ordinary slumber; float up to a \#near distance; turn someone invisible for a few minutes; create a mundane item from raw materials*
* **mag 2** — Major magic that strains the abilities of most practitioners, only masters can perform it reliably every day. Similar to 5e's Spell Levels 4 and 5, doing these things without magic would take extreme effort, coordination, tools, time, and/or training. Examples include: *shoot an explosive fireball (*1d12 dmg \#piercing*); heal broken bones* (1d12 HP restored)*; communicate with a ghost or spirit; summon an elemental servant; read someone's secret desire; charm a guarded or hostile mind; induce sleep that won't end until the next day; fly for the next few minutes; turn someone invisible for an hour; conjure a mundane item out of thin air*
* **mag 3** — High magic that is impossible for everyone but master practitioners. Equivalent to 5e's Spell Levels 6-7, it would be nearly impossible to do something similar without magic, even with all the help in the world. Example effects include: *emit a cloud of gas that poisons those who breath it* (1d12+1d6 dmg \#piercing)*; restore someone on the verge of death* (1d12+1d6 HP restored)*; communicate with another realm of existence; summon a demonic guardian; control a weakened mind; induce a year-long slumber; fly for a few hours; turn someone invisible for the rest of the day; transform one creature into another similar one; create a door to another dimension; telekinetically lift a boulder or small house; teleport a few kilometers*
* **mag 4+** — Mythical magic that some masters spend their entire lives in pursuit of, only the best of the best can accomplish these effects with a spell; most need to [**Perform a Ritual**](#perform-a-ritual) instead. Equivalent to 5e's Spell Levels 8 and 9, even with magic these feats are often only heard about in stories and legends. Example effects include: *utter a forbidden word of power that kills someone instantly or inflicts* (1d12+1d10 dmg \#piercing)*; resurrect someone from the dead; communicate through time and space; summon a monstrous behemoth; control an alien or guarded mind; induce an eternal slumber; fly forever; create a* magic item *or* Artifact*; teleport yourself and allies anywhere in the world*

### Comfort or Support {#comfort-or-support}

| Once per session, after you offer comfort or support to another PC, answer the following questions: *Did you explore, change, or reaffirm a Relationship or Bond? Did they open up to you in return? Did either of you reveal something new about your Struggle?* For each “Yes”, the comforted can choose to clear a marked Condition, gain 1 Depth to their Relationship with the comforter, or have the comforter clear a marked Condition. The comforted can choose an option more than once. |
| :---- |

If one or both of the PCs don't already have Relationships with the other, their Players have the option to create one now as part of this Move and answer “Yes” to the first question.

**Comfort or Support** can look like many different things. Maybe you play music for them; or cook together, or you duel to blow off some steam, or you just talk, or you just sit with them quietly.

This is the main way PCs clear Conditions and grow closer together. Each PC can only use the Move once per session (consolation only goes so far), but multiple PCs can **Comfort or Support** the same PC if they want, even at the same time.

### Defy Danger (+Variable) {#defy-danger-(+variable)}

| When you contend with a risk, threat, or obstacle—and no other Move fits—describe how you do it, then the GM chooses one and you roll: \+STR if you power through, physically endure, or break it \+DEX if you dodge, outpace, or target it precisely \+INT if you analyze, notice, or outsmart it \+WIS if you scrutinize, empathize with, or resolutely resist it \+CHA if you charm, trick, impress, or provoke it ✷On a 10+, you overcome it flawlessly. ✷On a 7-9, you prevail over it, but choose one below: The effort drains or rattles you; mark a Condition You draw unwanted attention or escalate the situation; the GM will say how after you choose this You sacrifice something to do it; say what it is. The GM may ask for more ✷On a 6-, your efforts are thwarted or go terribly wrong; mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. |
| :---- |

While this Move is very open-ended, it should still only be used when you “*contend with a risk, threat, or obstacle*”. If you are doing something more relaxed like fishing, cooking, chatting, or exploring (or if you're doing something adventurous but not *contending* with anything) then this Move won't trigger. In such instances when this Move doesn’t trigger, if you want to know how the situation resolves, look to the GM and they will follow the game’s Agenda and Principles to tell you what happens.

Sometimes, in order to bring another Move to bear, you might first need to overcome one or more obstacles between you and your target. This is usually handled through **Defy Danger**, but not always. Examples of such instances include: escaping a brawl long enough to [**Cast a Spell**](#cast-a-spell-\(+variable\)); pushing past a bodyguard to get to an enemy leader; chasing down a citizen to convince them you're not a monster; or impressing a queen enough for her to grant you an audience.

The GM may say that some approaches don't make sense for some dangers. Most people can't talk their way through a hall of spike traps, precisely target invisible poison gas, or power through incinerating dragonfire. If your approach doesn't work, the GM will tell you. If you double-down, they might just inflict the consequences upon you.

### Pull Strings (+CHA) {#pull-strings-(+cha)}

| When you influence an NPC to do what you want, choose an approach below and roll+CHA: If you *deceive them with false words or actions*, they might later seek the truth or learn a secret about you If you *persuade them openly and honestly*, they might ask for a promise and/or payment, or only partly follow through If you *threaten their safety or interests*, they might escalate the conflict now, or escape or betray you later ✷On a 10+, they'll do it as best as they can. ✷On a 7-9, they'll try, but the GM will add a complication based on your approach. ✷On a 6-, they make things worse; mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. |
| :---- |

This Move isn't mind control. NPCs have their own wants and drives, and can't easily be influenced to act against those. *Persuading* an NPC usually has the lowest risk, but the NPC has to care about your argument in the first place for it to work. *Intimidation or deception* tend to work on a lot more NPCs, but when they go wrong, the consequences tend to be more hostile.

Each approach's complications are suggestions for the GM, but if something else fits the situation better, they’re free to use that instead. No matter what, the complications should feel earned by the NPC's drive and the PCs past and present actions.

### Sense Motive (+WIS) {#sense-motive-(+wis)}

| When you scrutinize someone throughout a conversation, roll+WIS. ✷On a 10+, you can ask their player two questions during the conversation (if they’re an NPC, the GM answers). ✷On a 7-9, ask them only one. These questions can be about a character’s thoughts, feelings, or motivations. All answers must be truthful, even if the character(s) might not be. ✷On a 6-, if studying an NPC, then you're discovered or interrupted; mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. If studying a PC, then they ask you a question instead. |
| :---- |

Some example questions include:

* *Are you lying about \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_?*
* *How might I get you to \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_?*
* *How do you feel about \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_?*
* *What do you desire most?*

As Players and GM, always ask and answer these questions plainly and clear. In character, however, you can use subtext and inference to create powerful dramatic dialogue. Maybe you ask something out loud, or maybe you assess their posture and judge their intent. Perhaps a character says one answer out loud, but their eyes say a different one, and they might not even know it themselves.

While all answers must be truthful, they don't need to be comprehensive. Your person-to-person answers should have as much detail as feels appropriately dramatic, and be sensible to how the question was handled in the fiction. Quietly studying someone's behaviour might reveal that they feel immensely guilty, maybe even that the guilt is related to family. Getting them to open up to you might reveal that they accidentally killed their sibling, and that guilt has driven them for years.

### Sneak Past (+DEX) {#sneak-past-(+dex)}

| When you stealthily evade notice, roll+DEX. ✷On a 10+, you remain completely undetected. ✷On a 7-9, you do it, but the GM chooses one: Someone grows suspicious; you have dis to Sneak Past until that changes You did it by the skin of your teeth; mark a Condition You don’t have long; act now before the window of opportunity closes ✷On a 6-, you’re spotted or expected; mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. |
| :---- |

Setting up an ambush, picking a lock, emptying a pocket, or hiding among a crowd—these are just a few ways to employ this Move. It generally only triggers if you haven't been noticed yet. If you *have* been noticed, then you'll need to find a good hiding spot first.

### Spout Lore (+Questions) {#spout-lore-(+questions)}

| When you rack your brain for information about a specific subject, ask the GM one question about it, answer the questions below, then roll with \+1 for each “Yes”: *Do you have access to a book or equivalent record about this subject? Is this subject related to your [Origins](#origins)? Is this subject relevant to your [Struggle](#struggles)?* ✷On a 10+, the GM answers generously, and you or an ally also gain adv the first time someone acts on the answer. ✷On a 7-9, the GM just answers generously. ✷On a 6-, the GM answers with danger or an unwelcome truth; mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. |
| :---- |

Everyone has their own past experiences with the world. While many other fantasy TTRPGs have you rely on your Intelligence or similar to recall information, here we mostly ask how central it is to your character.

If you've never had a [**Struggle**](#struggles), then the answer to the third question is always “No”. If it's relevant to an already-resolved Struggle, then the answer can still be “Yes”.

Some examples questions include:

* *Where is \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ located?*
* *What could have caused \_\_\_\_\_\_\_ ?*
* *What might be the best way to accomplish \_\_\_\_\_\_\_?*
* *What have I heard or read about \_\_\_\_\_\_\_?*
* *How might I gain access to \_\_\_\_\_\_\_?*

### Trade Blows (+STR or \+DEX) {#trade-blows-(+str-or-+dex)}

| When you engage a foe in battle, roll+STR in melee or \+DEX at range (or with \#precise weapons). ✶On a 10+, choose one: An ally gets a fleeting opportunity You inflict max dmg You suffer min dmg ✶ On a 7-9, choose one of these instead: Something upsets or provokes you; mark a Condition You grant the opponent a fleeting opportunity You lose or break something. Choose what; the GM may ask for more ✶ On a 6-, you inflict min dmg or suffer max dmg (your call); mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. After choices have been made, inflict your dmg and suffer theirs. |
| :---- |

**Trading Blows** represents a back-and-forth exchange of attacks from multiple sides in a fight. When it doesn’t make narrative sense for dmg to be suffered or inflicted to both combatants, then double-check if this Move triggers at all. If your foe can't hurt you back, then just deal damage (or defeat them, GM's call). If you can't hurt them, maybe you suffer damage and must take a new approach or get into a better position.

Choices here are made *before* dmg is rolled. You don't get to roll a 1 on dmg dealt and then choose to change it to max dmg afterwards.

### Unearth Secrets (+INT) {#unearth-secrets-(+int)}

| When you investigate a mysterious situation or location, ask the GM, *Are there any secrets to discover here?* If “Yes”, roll+INT. No matter what you roll, you always discover something; the GM will tell you what. ✷On a 10+, your discovery is clear and significant. ✷On a 7-9, choose one from below: It is an important clue, but not a clear answer The discovery puts you at risk; the GM will say how after you choose this option The discovery rattles you; mark a Condition ✷On a 6-, the discovery is dangerous; mark 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. |
| :---- |

You should only be rolling this Move if the GM answers “Yes” to the initial question. If there isn't anything  to discover, then the GM just says “No” and the conversation progresses normally.

“*Investigating*” requires much more active, hands-on activity than just glancing around and taking in your surroundings. You might be running your fingers along the bricks in a wall to find a hidden door, or interviewing witnesses to a recent murder, or putting your ear to a door to hear a muffled conversation. If you just want to know what you perceive around you, ask the GM and they will answer.

## Extra Moves {#extra-moves}

These are Moves that will usually be triggered less often than [**Core**](#core-moves) ones, and are heavily dependent on the comings and goings of each specific campaign and playgroup. The frequency with which you use them in play can even vary from session to session.

### End the Session {#end-the-session}

| When you reach the end of a session, answer the following questions as a group: *Did anyone add Depth to their Relationship(s)? Did we defeat a dangerous threat, visit a new location, and/or find a memorable treasure? Did we learn something new and important about the world or NPCs?* For each “Yes”, everyone marks XP. |
| :---- |

For the first question, it's important to clarify that if *anyone* added Depth to one of their Relationships, *everyone* in the group gains one XP.

If it's not clear if a threat counts as “dangerous”, a location counts as “new”, etc., discuss it as a group and come to an agreement. If you can't reach a consensus, the GM has final say.

### Face Death {#face-death}

| When you reach 0 HP, you’re Helpless until you recover HP or the Scene ends. While Helpless, if you lose any HP, you die. When you recover any HP, lock one of your Conditions and you're no longer Helpless. When you take decisive action while Helpless, choose one before resolving the action: *Gamble with Death*; roll+nothing. ✷On a 10+, you push past the pain; recover half your max HP (rounded down) then resolve your action. ✷On a 7-9, the GM says what part of you won't survive; accept the loss and recover 1 HP, or hold on to it and die after acting. ✷On a 6-, you're too hurt to act and will die before this Scene ends; you say when. *What are your last words?* *Go Out in a Blaze of Glory*; briefly take control of the Scene, describe one last deed you accomplish, then die. |
| :---- |

While Helpless you may or may not be unconscious, but you can't feasibly do anything big (i.e. most Moves) without it counting as “decisive action”. Maybe you can talk, but not eloquently enough to [**Pull Strings**](#pull-strings-\(+cha\)). Maybe you can remember things, but you can't wrack your brain enough to [**Spout Lore**](#spout-lore-\(+questions\)).

When “*taking brief control of the Scene”*, you get to describe how your character overcomes all challenges in their last moments. You're still responsible for keeping it sane and somewhat feasible—don't randomly fly into the sun and turn it into a unicorn—but, beyond that, it's up to you what feels satisfying to your character to accomplish in death.

### Level Up {#level-up}

| When you mark the last XP on your PC’s Experience track, clear the track, add 1 to your Level and current/maximum HP (up to \+10), and gain one Advancement available to your new Level (or lower). |
| :---- |

You can **Level Up** in the middle of a Scene, or you can save the Advancement itself for after the session (when you have time to look at the options).

### Make Camp {#make-camp}

| Once per session, when you spend a few hours resting (even while adventuring), describe cooperatively what each PC is up to. Things in the world worsen each time you Make Camp. When you finish Making Camp, each PC regains all HP and clears one marked Condition. |
| :---- |

Feel free to discuss situations, events, or emotions while **Making Camp**. Not all conversations have to be in a group; one person can go off on their own or pull an ally aside for a 1-on-1. Other Moves might happen while you **Make Camp**, such as [**Comfort or Support**](#comfort-or-support) or [**Sense Motive**](#sense-motive-\(+wis\)).

“*Things in the world worsen*” means that the Threats and other dangers in the fantasy world creep closer to their goals. Sometimes this manifests in obvious ways, other times it may be more subtle and require investigation. Urgent events may even interrupt this Move.

If the Move is interrupted, you need to resume and finish it to get its benefits.

### Perform a Ritual {#perform-a-ritual}

| When you draw on a font of magical power (object, person, or place) to create an effect, tell the GM what you’re trying to achieve. The GM will tell you a number of requirements up to the effect’s mag (min 1\) from the following (filling in the blanks or making choices when needed): *First you must \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ It’s going to take days/weeks/months It will require \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ The effect will be unreliable or limited You and your allies will risk \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ You’ll need help from \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ You’ll have to destroy \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_ to do it* Once all requirements are fulfilled, the ritual is complete and the magical effect takes place. |
| :---- |

Any PC can **Perform a Ritual**. Even if you can't **Cast a Spell**, you can still draw on a font of power to accomplish effects that might be impossible otherwise. It's up to the GM what counts as a suitable font of power.

### Reach Your Limit {#reach-your-limit}

| When you mark your last Condition, clear all your marked Conditions then choose and act out one of the following outbursts: Angry — *Lash out at your allies* or *break something important* Distracted — *Wander off alone* or *miss something relevant* Exhausted — *Collapse* or *give up when it matters* Frightened — *Hide* or *flee from danger* Insecure — *Overcompensate* or *harshly criticize someone* If your outburst isn't extreme enough, the GM may ask you to go further. After the outburst, lock the chosen Condition. |
| :---- |

**Reaching Your Limit** is a great way to clear Conditions—as long as you're willing to accept the consequences of the outburst (and a single locked Condition afterwards).

### Undertake a Perilous Journey {#undertake-a-perilous-journey}

| When you travel through hostile territory, choose a destination, a leader, and a method of travel: *Quickly, risking it all* — \-1 to the roll, everyone marks a Condition; you arrive earlier than expected *Regular pace* — \+1 to the roll, only the leader marks a Condition; you arrive when expected *Slow & careful* — \+2 to the roll; you arrive later than expected Then the leader rolls with the corresponding modifier. ✷On a 10+, you arrive with a boon; the GM will tell you what. ✷On a 7-9, you’ll get there but choose one: Someone’s following you; let them, or everyone marks a Condition to lose them Something unpleasant awaits at your destination You encounter a danger along the way, but you have the drop on them ✷On a 6-, a danger surprises you; everyone marks 1 XP and the GM makes a Move. |
| :---- |

You need to have a specific destination in mind for this Move to trigger. If you're just wandering, exploring, or surveying the land, then either another Move might trigger (**Unearth Secrets** maybe) or the GM will just make a Move and say what happens.

You also have to be traveling through “*hostile territory*”, which might be an enemy country, dangerous forest, monster-infested cave network, or bandit-laden road. The GM has the final say on what territory counts as “hostile”.

A "boon" might be a valuable ruin, a helpful NPC, a magical tree, an abandoned treasure, or something else. Even if it isn't immediately obviously useful, with some hard work and/or smart ideas it can still pay off.

# Running the Game {#running-the-game}

## Behind the Screen {#behind-the-screen}

Running a TTRPG session can be *hard*; anybody who’s ever done it knows that. But it doesn't *have to be*, which is why we added so much to this section. One of the biggest strengths of this style of game (PbtA) is its inherent flexibility and freedom, and we want this section to leverage those strengths and make **Dungeon World 2** as easy and fun to run as possible.

While previous sections of these rules are in the style of an instruction manual, this one is more like a letter and toolbox from us, as designers, to you, the GM. You want to have great experiences at the table, and we want to help, so let's talk about what we think that means.

There are also lots of tools in this chapter, meant to help both in-the-moment improvisation and structured preparation. Please pick and choose which ones you want to use. While some may reference each other, they're mostly standalone in nature.

Thank you for running this game for your friends, family, or even strangers\! We sincerely hope that everything you find here helps you to have the most enjoyment while orchestrating, encouraging, and growing your Players’ fun. **Dungeon World 2** truly couldn’t exist without you\!

*—Helena & Spencer*

## The Role of the GM {#the-role-of-the-gm}

Being a Game Master (GM) is an important part of the game, and it can be extremely fun and rewarding, but it can also be nerve-wracking when first starting out (or even after decades of experience). To help ease those pressures, here is our list of what a GM *is* and (more importantly) *isn't* responsible for:

* **You are responsible for portraying the world around the PCs**, such as the environment, NPCs, enemies, and obstacles all along the way.
* **Your NPCs are NOT the protagonists**. They can still be awesome, some can be around frequently, but their most memorable moments should draw the game's focus back to the PCs in some way.
* **You are NOT responsible for everyone’s fun**. This is a collaborative game, and everyone is equally responsible for being mature, kind, and respectful to each other.
* **You are here to have fun too\!** Collaboration is a two-way street, so feel free to inject your ideas into the world, or suggest an idea to a Player (after asking if they are okay with you doing so, of course).
* **You are NOT the “author of a story”**. This is a shared game about an evolving conversation that creates something like a story *ex post facto*. If you go in with a fixed “plot” in mind, then we assure you everything will become more difficult and less fun for everyone. Lean into the unexpected and let yourself be surprised. PLAY TO FIND OUT\!
* **You are NOT the organizer**. It’s up to everyone in the group to decide how to handle things like session scheduling, group behavior, snacks, and what to do if someone is late or can’t make it. Don’t overburden yourself with these tasks, and learn to delegate them as much as possible.
* **You are NOT infallible**, so don’t sweat over the small stuff. If you make a mistake, or have trouble coming up with ideas, that’s okay. Feel free to ask for help from others in your gaming group, or outside of it.

## Play Order Flowchart {#play-order-flowchart}

This showcases what the flow of a game of **Dungeon World 2** typically looks like, in more detailed fashion than in the Playing the Game section, and focused on the GM’s side of things.

1. **You as the GM make a Move**. Following the game’s [**Principles**](#principles), you set up a situation. Then you usually ask “*What do you do?*”
2. **Player(s) describe their response**. Sometimes you or another Player might ask clarifying questions about this response, such as “*What does it look like when you do that?*” or “*What do you want to accomplish?*”
3. **Is a Move triggered?** If so, proceed to \#3. If not, go back to \#1.
4. **Is there a roll involved?** If not, resolve the Move and then go back to the start. If so, roll what the Move says. Freely remind the PCs they can **Aɪᴅ** each other. Also, remember that Conditions affect rolls.
5. **What was the roll’s result?** Follow what the Move says happens.

* A 10 or higher (10+) means a full success, as close to clean as can be expected
* A 7, 8, or 9 (7-9) is still a success but messier, with a cost, drawback, or milder consequence
* A 6 or lower (6-) means things go badly. Most of the time that means the GM makes a Move and the rolling Player gains XP, but a few Moves say something else.

1. **What now?** After every Move’s resolved you need to update the fiction to reflect the change that happened.
2. **Has any PC marked all Conditions or reached 0 HP?** If so, they [**Reach Their Limit**](#reach-your-limit) (for Conditions) or [**Face Death**](#face-death) (if they reached 0 HP).
3. Go back to \#1\!

## Agenda {#agenda}

These are the core overarching goals you have while running a game of **Dungeon World 2**. Even if you forget or miss other parts of this chapter, the Agenda is what you should refer to when in need of guidance. Your Agenda every session is:

* Fill the Player Characters' lives with adventure
* Play to find out what happens
* Portray a meaningful fantasy world

### Fill the Player Characters' lives with adventure

By “life of adventure” we mean one of highs and lows, victories and defeats, drinks with friends, hard roads, swollen bruises, and open laughter. Give the PCs opportunities (and motives) to accomplish exciting things, but don't make it too easy (or it won’t be adventurous). We recommend you put the Player Characters in situations that test them, hurt them, exploit their weaknesses, show off their strengths, and/or tempt their desires… Then see how they handle it all. We encourage you to celebrate their successes, mourn their losses, and anticipate—as best as you can—what they might do next.

You may need to prepare (or “prep” in TTRPG parlance) the ingredients of an adventure ahead of time, such as:

* ideas for [**Potential Scenes**](#outline-other-potential-scenes)
* details (and stats when necessary) for [**Important NPCs & Villains**](#develop-important-npcs)
* descriptions for some [**Fantastic Locations**](#detail-fantastic-locations) and, of course,
* some [**Rewards**](#prepare-rewards)**.**

If you want more guidance about how to Prep a Session, check [here](#preparing-a-session-cheat-sheet).

If you prefer to improvise, at least knowing that these ingredients are important to create an adventure should help you with coming up with them in the moment.

### Play to Find Out What Happens

The key difference between a TTRPG and any other type of game or fiction is that in these games—and DW2 is no different—**no one knows what will happen in advance**. You as the GM may do some setup, but the Players and their PCs will interact with your preparation and change the situation, often in ways you can't predict. On the flip side, every time the PCs attempt something, they’re confronted with the fictional world that you portray and its often unexpected (especially to them) consequences.

This back and forth creates a wonderful unknown that is discovered, through play, by the whole table together. This is what we mean with **play to find out what happens**\! We invite you to embrace this uncertainty, and to empower the Players to do the same. So, don't hold on to your prep or ideas too hard, because they'll probably never come true in *exactly* the way you imagined them. Similarly, Players shouldn't really grow too attached to their plans or even their PCs, because something could always happen that changes everything.

### Portray a Meaningful Fantasy World

Fantasy fiction and games—and DW2 is no different—include many elements that do not and could never exist in our world, such as magic, nonhuman sapient beings, and other dimensions that can’t usually be accessed physically, among many others. “Fantasy” may seem like an obvious concept these days, but such worlds have become so filled with mundanity as to not feel truly fantastical anymore.

We believe that what makes fantasy worlds amazing is the right mixture of *wonder* and *believability*. If you focus on wonder too much, the world (and game) often feels inconsistent and driven by whim, which can reduce the emotional impact of events and consequences. On the other hand, focusing purely on believability will make the world feel predictable, sterile, or boring— too close to what we are already familiar with. As such, we recommend you don't try to rationalize or unnecessarily justify the fantastical elements in the world, and instead leave room for new and peculiar forms of the impossible to appear later.

The abstract ideal lies in a balance between the two. The specific proportions will depend on you and your group’s preferences, so you’ll probably need to discuss this with them during [**Session Zero**](#session-zero) procedures, but here are some ideas for adding both to your world.

To make your world more wondrous:

* *Incorporate unique aspects of your world* into its factions, traditions, and civilizations
* *Introduce something unique and magical*, such as a location, creature, or phenomenon
* *Make the world’s environments vibrant*, with dangerous inhabitants and colorful flora and fauna

To make your world feel more believable:

* *Describe what the PCs see and hear, yes, but also what they smell, touch, and more*. How warm is the fire? What does the room smell like after a magical lightning is unleashed within it?
* *Give its inhabitants human emotions and struggles*. Maybe elves don't sleep or age, but they still struggle with love, loss, and fear, even if they might express it in unusual ways.
* *Show how the passage of time affects people and places*. This could be as simple as a holiday festival, or a change in leadership, an ongoing project, or a collapsing building. Change, growth, and decay all overlap

## Principles {#principles}

If the Agenda are your objectives/goals as a GM, then the Principles are your ways/roads of getting there, your specific guidelines on how to achieve them. In short, they are:

* Be a fan of the Players & their characters
* Describe with dramatic detail
* Give every NPC a motivation
* Let the Players roleplay with each other
* Use the PCs’ names whenever possible

### Be a Fan of the Players and Their Characters

While authentically portraying the world (and its consequences) may lead you to harm the PCs, you are neither an “antagonistic enemy” to them nor an “impartial referee” of their actions. We envision you, the GM, as someone who’s purposefully on the side of the PCs, collaborating with the Players to go on adventures and have fun at the table.

Going even beyond that, we recommend that you try and be a fan of the Players at your table. This should lead you, hopefully, to create a cooperative environment in which everyone feels comfortable speaking up, offering their own ideas, and being creative. We dearly encourage you to incorporate the Players’ ideas into the game as much as you can, even if they seem weird or discordant at first. In short: try to “Yes, and…” as much as possible, and reserve your “No”s to the moments when you really need them.

### Describe with Dramatic Detail

When describing characters, environments, and actions, we invite you to aim for vivid, dynamic language. A yeti doesn’t just swing its claws—it roars, grabs its victim, and drags them towards their frozen lair. A burning acid swamp doesn't just bubble corrosively; the air burns your nostrils as clouds of colorful insects bite you.

It takes practice to become good at this, but often the best way to start is to **focus on a few specific details and let the Players’ imaginations fill in the rest**. If you describe a brilliant apothecary with thick glasses, a simmering scowl, swarmed by a dozen scrambling assistants, then that’s more than enough to convey a strong impression of her.

In contrast, we think that the best way to communicate larger scale situations is in down-to-earth ways. If there is a plague sweeping through a city, then maybe evoke the feeling of decaying streets, describe the smell of burning bodies, and have a coughing beggar ask for change.

### Give every NPC a motivation

Non-player characters (NPCs) are your characters in the game. As such, the best advice we can offer on how to portray them better is to *know why they do what they do*. In writing, this is often referred to as a character’s motivation, desire, or goal. We use the term **Drive** in our NPC statblocks. The idea is the same: knowing a character’s underlying reason for acting the way they do will make it easier for you to roleplay them.  Additionally, if the PCs come to learn that reason, it will add a sense of believability to the fantasy world you’re all building together.

### Let the Players roleplay with each other

One of the most common foibles among GMs is that *we just speak too much*. We want to “fill the silence,” so to speak, to keep things moving, and to make the whole game as entertaining as possible for everyone involved. The “problem” is that part of the fun of playing a TTRPG is roleplaying PC-PC interactions when you are a Player. DW2, even more so, has as a core part of its vision the idea that PCs are encouraged to form deep Bonds with each other. And even though that may happen mechanically without that much actual roleplay, the ideal is that the Players feel that their characters are getting closer to one another in the fiction.

So, in short: every once in a while just shut up, sit back, and enjoy your Players roleplaying with each other. Let it happen naturally, and don’t intervene unless you’re required to. The adventure will be ready when they are.

### Use the PCs’ names whenever possible

If you’re running the game for people you know, it may feel weird or awkward to refer to them by invented fantasy names. As true as that may be, though, if you talk to them by using their real names, you’re referring to them as Players outside of the fiction, and that isn’t a good way to encourage them to roleplay their PCs. Instead, learn by heart and use the PCs’ names as much as you can, so Players are subtly pushed to answer questions and look at things in the fiction from their PC’s perspective which may, in some cases, greatly differ from their own.

## GM Moves {#gm-moves}

These Moves are the way in which you as the GM add to the conversation, altering the fiction directly. This is one of the ways in which the role of GM differs from that of a Player: the Players need to trigger Moves (and usually roll dice) to alter the fiction, while you just get to make a Move, either when instructed or when you think it’s appropriate.

### When to Make a GM Move

Generally you should make a GM Move when…

* A Player rolls a 6- on a Move
* The Players look to you for a response or answer
* You have a golden opportunity

#### A Player rolls a 6- on a Move

This one’s easy. All Moves in the game that include dice-rolling on the part of the Players—and thus can produce a 6- result—will tell you on the Move’s text that you get to make a GM Move as a result. Which GM Move will depend on the specific situation, but the most important decision is whether the Move will be “hard” or “soft” (see [**Hard & Soft Moves**](#hard-&-soft-moves) for more info).

#### The Players look to you for a response or answer

This one is often automatic. The Players want to know what will happen next, and they look at you to portray the world to them. *How does the sleeping troll react to the spell's magic? What's behind the secret door they just opened?* Make a GM Move and describe what happens in response to their actions.

Sometimes a moment may be tense enough that you might think dice should be rolled. Check if any available PC Moves apply, but if none do then the tension is yours to resolve with a GM Move.

#### You have a golden opportunity

If the players do something that is an exceptionally bad (or good) idea, they're giving you a golden opportunity to make an appropriate GM Move, even if you interrupt them or override something. For example, if a PC swings their weapon by jumping into a dragon's open mouth, then maybe instead of triggering [**Trade Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\)) they just take damage or are outright swallowed. On the other hand, if they offer an NPC the very jewel they've been secretly longing for, then maybe they don't need to **Pull Strings**, as the NPC may just agree to their offer.

### Hard & Soft Moves {#hard-&-soft-moves}

A GM Move can be described as “hard” or “soft” based on the severity of its consequences, and how much the PCs can do to negate or recover from it.

#### Hard Moves

A **hard Move** is when **something changes in the fiction in a way that can’t easily be undone**, such as an ally getting seriously wounded, a treasure getting lost or stolen, or an enemy escaping the PC’s grasp. Hard Moves are typically described with the full consequences, with the PCs only being asked “*What do you do*?” **after** this happens, rather than before or during the resolution of the Move.

For example: *“The elementalist raises a crackling orb high in one hand, and even as you dive for her, a bolt of lightning arcs straight into the market center, detonating several barrels in a fiery explosion”*.  In this case, the unrecoverable consequence here is the explosion.

#### Soft Moves

On the other hand, a **soft Move lets the PCs intervene before the consequences are fully in effect**. Maybe you describe a sword swinging towards a PC, but they still have a chance to dodge or block it, or you ominously hint at strange lights coming from the sealed tower in the center of town, giving the PCs a chance to investigate. Soft Moves are often used to warn the PCs of future Hard Moves, sometimes imminent ones and sometimes distant.

For example: *“The duchess pauses her speech to look at each face in the crowd. You suppress goosebumps as her gaze meets yours, before moving on to the next guest. It seems she’s heard rumors of your plan.”* The soft Move here is that the PCs may be under suspicion.

### GM Core Moves {#gm-core-moves}

Other Powered by the Apocalypse (PbtA) games present sets of GM Moves that are highly evocative and specific, describing as many Moves available to the GM as possible. In DW2 we’ve decided for a more didactic approach, if you will. We assume you’ve never run another PbtA before and, as such, our list of GM Moves is less extensive and more general, trying to point to the most fundamental actions a GM can take in more or less broad strokes.

To make the GM Moves as easy to remember and apply while running the game, we’ve further divided them into three main categories: GM Moves that **increase the tension**; GM Moves that **release the tension**; and GM Moves that **ask for a Player’s input**.

Within these three subdivisions, the specific GM Core Moves are:

#### Increase the tension

* Have one or more PCs pay a cost
* Make the situation worse
* Skip to the middle of the action

#### Release the tension

* Allow for a moment of respite
* Make the situation better

#### Ask for a Player’s input

* Ask someone to describe something
* Present the PCs with a decision

Here are the GM Core Moves described in detail.

#### Allow for a moment of respite

Especially after a moment of high tension—such as a protracted fight or intense negotiation/intrigue Scene—the Players will be looking forward to a moment to relax, [**Comfort or Support**](#comfort-or-support) each other, [**Make Camp**](#make-camp), or just talk to each other. It’s very important to grant them these moments because, otherwise, it may feel like too much.

#### Ask someone to describe something

The most important aspect of a TTRPG is that it is a collaborative effort. If you as the GM are the only one describing the fantasy world, then it becomes your (singular) world instead of your (plural) world. As a result, we advise you to ask the Players—especially those who are on the quieter side—to describe what they’re seeing, to add elements to a location, or to describe a specific quirk of an NPC. With some Players this may feel like pulling teeth at first but, with practice and patience, most Players in our experience end up enjoying these moments of creative spotlight greatly.

#### Have one or more PCs pay a cost

This can mean to have them mark a use of a piece of equipment, a Condition, or even to lose some precious HP. The important thing here is that PC(s) feel that the situation is getting more dangerous and difficult, so the specific cost will be highly dependent on whatever makes sense in the fiction at the time.

#### Make the situation better

If every twist in the fiction is bad, then they’ll stop being twists at all, and they’ll become predictable and, worst of all, boring. As such, nice twists are not only desirable but fundamental for good pacing. Having a villain surrender, an unexpected ally appear to help save the day, or just receiving good news can improve the mood of the Players for the better—and restore the enthusiasm after losing badly.

#### Make the situation worse {#make-the-situation-worse}

Tension increases when things turn from good to bad—or bad to worse—suddenly and unexpectedly, so that the shift in the fiction makes the PCs (and maybe even Players) pay more attention to what’s happening. An ally revealing a betrayal, a wound that turns out to be fatal, or a portcullis that suddenly separates the group are all good examples of this.

#### Present the PCs with a decision

If you want a situation to feel significant and important to the Players, explain what’s going on and offer them a decision. The more meaningful and difficult the decision, the more the Players will talk about it, invest time and energy, and the more involved they’ll be in seeing through whatever decision/plan of action they invest in.

#### Skip to the middle of the action

The temptation to describe everything in extreme detail is always there… And we recommend you resist it as much as possible, especially if things are relaxing and you want to increase the tension back up again. Putting the NPCs in an unexpectedly tense but believable situation, out of nowhere, will surely have the PCs jump to action.

## Preparing a Session Cheat Sheet {#preparing-a-session-cheat-sheet}

There may be as many ways to prepare a TTRPG Session as there are GMs, but there’s one we’ve found to be both effective and relatively easy. It’s taken from the fantastic (*sic*) [**Lazy GM's Resource Document**](https://slyflourish.com/lazy_gm_resource_document.html) by Michael E. Shea of [**SlyFlourish.com**](http://SlyFlourish.com)

### Review the Player Characters

The first step we recommend is to review the PCs. *What are their names? What do they want? What plays into their backgrounds? What do their Players enjoy?*

You might not even write anything down during this step, but reviewing the PCs helps refreshing them into your mind—and ensures that the rest of your preparation keeps them in consideration.

### Create a Strong Start Scene

How a game starts is likely the most important piece of prep you can do. Setting the stage for the Session determines a great deal about how the rest of the game will go. When you define where a game session starts, you figure out what's going on, what the initial focus of the session is, and how you can get as close to the action as possible. **When in doubt, start with a fight**. Example strong starts include:

#### Cities & Towns Strong Starts

1. A golem from a wizards' academy goes on a rampage
2. A group of cultists kindly ask for a sample of a character's blood
3. A hooded patron visits the characters, asking the characters to kill them in two days
4. A noble lord bumps into one of the characters and threatens to have them arrested
5. A riot draws the local watch away, whereupon a squad of hired killers descends on the characters
6. A scarred explorer offers to sell one of the characters a map to a site of a lost or stolen ancestral heirloom
7. Something slithers out of a nearby sewer
8. The campaign's main villain shows up and invites the characters for a drink
9. The characters interrupt bandits breaking into a shop
10. The local monarch is assassinated and a villain takes over the government

#### Dungeons, Caves & Caverns Strong Starts

1. A spectral hound guides the characters to the camp of a reclusive mage.
2. A vampire appears from a sudden rise of mist, introduces herself, and asks the characters for a favor
3. A wounded knight collapses near the characters, begging them to find her lost love before she dies
4. An ancient statue turns its head toward the characters and whispers a valuable secret
5. An eyestalk swells out from an oozy patch on the wall, beholds the characters, and then disappears back into the wall
6. Stars swim in a moonlit well, then rise up to reveal themselves as will-o'-wisps
7. The characters come across two bands of goblins fighting each other for the favor of a hag named Auntie Chiptooth
8. The floor collapses, revealing even deeper tunnels long forgotten
9. The ground cracks open and a pillar of chipped obsidian juts out, projecting a prophecy in red Infernal glyphs on the walls of the chamber
10. Through a cracked wall, the characters spot a gateway flanked by two huge obsidian statues, and featuring a set of stairs leading down

#### Wilderness Strong Starts

1. A golden-antlered stag leaps into the characters' camp and asks to be defended from the hunters chasing it
2. A nearby tree opens up, and a satyr steps through and says "Hi\!"
3. An old woman greets the characters, offering them candy and baked treats if they will come to her nearby cottage
4. A rampaging werebear storms through the area, mistaking the characters for the hunters who killed their mate
5. A sinkhole opens up, revealing the tunnels of long-forgotten burial chambers
6. A skeleton hanging from a tree begs the characters to right the wrong it committed while alive
7. Night falls, revealing an alien starscape above
8. The characters see a tall humanoid with antlers stalking from the shadows, carrying a large scythe in one hand and three humanoid heads in the other
9. The ground suddenly churns, bringing the body of a long-lost elf king to the surface. The king's eyes open
10. The characters stumble upon a nest of skeletal pixies surrounding a desecrated fey gate

### Outline Other Potential Scenes {#outline-other-potential-scenes}

With a strong start in hand, you can then outline a short list of other potential Scenes that might unfold. This step exists mostly to make you feel as though you have a handle on the game before you start. However, as GMs, all of us must always be ready to throw our potential Scenes away when the game goes in a different direction—as it often does. Usually, it's enough to come up with only a few words per Scene, and to expect one or two Scenes per hour of play. At other times, you might skip this step completely if you don't think you need it.

### Develop Important NPCs {#develop-important-npcs}

During prep, it’s best to outline those NPCs most critical to the Session, focusing on a name and a connection to the fiction, and stating them if necessary. Many other NPC —maybe even most of them—can be improvised right at the table.

### Detail Fantastic Locations {#detail-fantastic-locations}

In our experience, building evocative locations isn't easily improvised. As such, it's worth spending time writing out a handful of fantastic locations that the characters might discover and explore during the game. Each location can be thought of as a set, a room, or a backdrop for a single Scene in the Session.

Describe each location with a short evocative title such as “The Lost Citadel”. Then write down three descriptors for it, such as: *odor of stagnation and slow decay*; *stone as old as the mountains*; *strange echoes of sorrow and misery*.

Ultimately, whole dungeons can be built from a series of connected fantastic locations, with each location representing a large area or chamber. A specific location might not come up during the game for which you prepare it, but it will be ready for a later session as the PCs explore. Example locations include:

#### Starting Locations

Often a small settlement from which the PCs set out to explore neighboring lands. A village always works well as a starting location, but there are many alternatives.

1. Adventurers' guild
2. Crumbling fortress
3. Fortress under siege
4. Frontier outpost
5. Great library
6. Holy temple
7. Mining outpost
8. Planar hub city
9. Recent shipwreck
10. Refugee camp

#### Adventure Locations

We recommend you drop three of these into the areas close by the starting location

1. Abandoned dungeon

### Write Down Some Secrets & Clues

Secrets and clues are single short sentences that describe a clue, a piece of the story, or a piece of the world that the characters can discover during the game. **You shouldn’t have a fixed idea on how the characters will discover these clues**. As such, you'll want to keep these secrets and clues abstract from their place of discovery, so that you can drop them into the game wherever it makes sense. This lets the game flow freely, while still allowing you to reveal important pieces of the story at any point where the characters might discover them. During this step, you might write down ten such secrets or clues. Example secrets and clues include:

#### Historical Secrets

Use historical secrets to give the PCs meaningful and useful information as they explore the fantasy world. Secrets of this kind should provide characters and Players alike with bite-sized pieces of local or world history.

Historical secrets might be found as mosaics in ancient tombs, statues in old ruins, dusty tomes in ancient libraries, markings on strange weapons, or tales shared among elderly villagers.

1. What alien creature or power is hidden here?
2. What ancient civilization once thrived here?
3. What armies once battled here?
4. What cruel lord was slain in this place?
5. What dead god has a connection to the area?
6. What horrific monster once ruled here?
7. What old empire's settlements lie buried here?
8. What primeval mysteries lay buried here?
9. What rebellion took place here?
10. What was this location's former purpose?

#### NPC Secrets

Use NPC secrets to reveal information about these NPCs to the PCs, especially as a means of introducing villains before they face them.

PCs might learn NPC secrets from a herald or sidekick, rumors at a local pub, recovered journals, a minion's last words, captured letters, or town gossip.

1. What dark history follows the NPC?
2. What does the NPC desire?
3. What foe did the NPC defeat?
4. What great power does the NPC possess?
5. What makes the NPC politically untouchable?
6. What makes the NPC think they're right?
7. What regular routines does the NPC follow?
8. What secret does the NPC want to keep hidden?
9. What was the NPC's great accomplishment?
10. Who does the NPC love above all others?

#### Player Character Secrets

Use PCs secrets to tie them to the fantasy world. These sorts of secrets might be revealed by NPCs, old journals or letters, suddenly recalled memories, or prophetic dreams.

1. How is the villain related to the character?
2. What dreams fill the character's rest?
3. What family history might be revealed?
4. What ghost or spirit haunts the character?
5. What NPC who the character thinks is dead still lives?
6. What parasite secretly infests the character?
7. What previous event ties the character to the story?
8. What ritual was the character blessed with as a child?
9. What ties the character to this location?
10. Which family member is involved in the adventure?

#### Rest of the World Secrets

Use rest of the world secrets to inform the PCs about the larger events happening in the rest world (and to plant seeds for future Threats in Campaign Mode).

PCs might learn these secrets from quest-giving NPCs, notes found on defeated foes, dreams or portents from the gods, NPCs fleeing a disaster, arcane feedback from an object, or psychic projections.

1. What armies just invaded the realm?
2. What dark sign or portent just appeared?
3. What disaster is about to befall the land?
4. What dungeon entrance just became revealed?
5. What monsters recently appeared in the realm?
6. What natural disaster has recently struck the area?
7. What royal figure was just assassinated?
8. What unnatural being has appeared in the world?
9. What unusual creature was seen walking the wilds?
10. What villainous event will soon come to pass?

### Prepare Rewards {#prepare-rewards}

Players love treasure, and it's worthwhile to spend time preparing things they'll find interesting. This step also helps to directly impact the characters—by dropping an interesting part of the fiction literally into their hands. You can use a mixture of techniques to prepare these rewards, from generating them randomly to selecting specific items based on the PCs Classes and Paths and the desires of the Players. [**Treasure**](#treasure-&-wealth) pieces are also a great mechanism for delivering secrets and clues.

## Major NPCs {#major-npcs}

A Non-Player Character (NPC) is any character that the GM portrays: friends, foes, people, animals, monsters, spirits, or strangers. Each session typically involves various NPCs across different Scenes, which act as the most active part of the adventurer's surroundings.

All NPCs have the following traits:

* A **Name**, maybe of them individually, maybe just of the type of NPC they are collectively, plus what pronouns they use (if applicable)
* A **Description** that summarizes who they are
* A **Drive** that they primarily desire and pursue, be it an instinct, value, emotion, or vow
* A short list of **Resources** that they typically rely on to pursue and achieve their Drive

Additionally, an NPC may have all or any of these traits:

* If they have notable defenses or preparations against something, they might **Resist** some things, meaning they are unaffected by them by default (though this may be overcome narratively)
* If they know how to fight, then they’ll have **HP**, **dmg**, and **Armor**
* If they're a relevant NPC in the fiction, usually a major antagonist or obstacle, they'll have **Conditions** that cause **Escalations** when marked. Each Condition can be emotional or situational, and don't need to be the same as the PCs’ Conditions. When an NPC suffers significant dmg or setback (your call as the GM), you can mark a Condition and have them **Escalate**, pulling the spotlight back to them and [**Making the Situation Worse**](#make-the-situation-worse) in the process.

### Fighting NPCs

When an NPC inflicts dmg, usually from a PC [**Trading Blows**](#trade-blows-\(+str-or-+dex\)) with them, the GM rolls the dmg openly. When an NPC suffers dmg, reduce their HP by that same amount (just as PCs do).

An NPC is defeated when they either reach 0 HP or mark all of their Conditions (after or as part of their final Escalation). NPCs who aren't tough in a fight generally have no HP or dmg, meaning they are defeated after taking a single hit, or even without rolling.

When time passes, such as when the PCs [**Make Camp**](#make-camp), then NPCs can restore some or all of their lost HP and clear their Conditions. You can also restore HP or Conditions in the moment if the fiction fits, such as incredible regeneration abilities, but this should be rare and exceptional.

*\[More text will be added here later to talk about allies, hirelings, and managing multiple different NPCs in a fight\]*

### Creating an NPC Cheat Sheet

1. *Who are they and what do they look like?* Write as much or as little of this down as their **Description**
2. *What core goal or desire do they protect or advance?* Write that down as their **Drive**
3. *What do they use to pursue their Drive?* These could be approaches, tools, methods, weapons, or other things; write these down as their **Resources**

   *For a dragon this could be* teeth, claws, and fire*. A merchant might use* an always-friendly face, an eye for appraisal, *and* connections with everyone

4. *What are they particularly defended against or prepared for?* Write this down as what they **Resist**

   *A knight with a tower shield might* **Resist:** arrows*, while a master ranger might* **Resist:** people sneaking up on them in the wilderness

5. *How relevant do you expect them to be to the fiction?* Give them an appropriate number of Conditions and Escalations following these guidelines:

* *Barely a character at all* — 0 Conditions & Escalations
* *Minor character with a single trait* — 1 Condition & Escalation
* *Relevant character who spends time in the spotlight* — 2-3 Conditions & Escalations
* *Critically relevant character, candidate to be a major antagonist to the PCs* — 4-5 Conditions & Escalations

1. *Do they know how to fight?* If “Yes”, continue on. If not, you're done\!

2. *How do they usually fight?* This determines their **starting dmg** and **HP**.

* *In large groups* — d4 dmg, 3 HP
* *In small groups, about 2–5* — d6 dmg, 6 HP
* *All by its lonesome* — d8 dmg, 12 HP

1. *How big are they?*

* *Smaller than a house cat* — \#hand, reduce [dmg die size](#damage-dice-size) by one step
* *Between halfling and human sized* — \#close \#hand
* *As big as a cart* — \#close \#hand \#near, \+4 HP, increase [dmg die size](#damage-dice-size) by one step
* *Much larger than a cart* — \#close \#far \#hand \#near, \+8 HP, increase [dmg die size](#damage-dice-size) by two steps
* *Can devour ships or houses whole* — \#close \#far \#near, \+12 HP, increase [dmg die size](#damage-dice-size) by three steps

1. *What is their most important defense?*

* *Cloth or flesh* — Armor 0
* *Leathers or thick hide* — Armor 1
* *Mail or scales* — Armor 2
* *Plate or bone* — Armor 3
* *Permanent magical protection* — Armor 4 and/or list what it Resists

 1. *Which of these describes them?* Choose all that apply:

* *Abhors violence or especially frail* — reduce damage die size by one step
* *Actively defends themselves with a shield or similar* — \+1 armor
* *Usually attacks at range* — \#near \#far or both (your call)
* *Especially deadly* — increase damage die size by one step
* *Prone to extreme emotion or outbursts* — \+1 Condition & Escalation

#### Infamous Thief (pro/nouns)

*Friend or foe? Hard to know with a face-covering mask*

## Threats {#threats}

Threats are antagonistic forces that endanger what the PCs care about, drive them to adventure, and challenge them during play. They may be things that will happen in the future or things happening right now, but either way the PCs probably want to stop them from happening.

Threats are made up of several components:

* **Name** — What the Threat is. This could be: *an infesting curse, an ambitious organization, a corrupting influence, an encroaching army, an invading plane, a collapsing society, an awakening titan*, etc.
* **Description** — A short sentence adding context and details
* **Goal** — How the Threat will change the world for the worse, intentionally or not
* **Assets** — Advantages it has going for it before it's introduced, such as important locations, NPCs, objects, secrets, or even stranger things. During play the PCs may learn about these Assets and act to remove them from the Threat's control
* **Developments** — Steps the Threat will enact to reach towards its Goal. Once enough Developments are completed, the goal is achieved. Maybe all Developments need to be completed, maybe just some of them, depending on the Threat and the fiction.
* **Reactions** — Actions the Threat might take during a session in response to the PCs impeding it, often as a negative consequence or GM Move

Optionally, a Threat may also have:

* **Scenes** that may happen in relation to the Threat
* **Secrets** that can be discovered or revealed about the Threat

### Developments

Developments are meant to prompt Scenes during the game, usually ones involving tension and action regarding what terrible things might happen next. Generally, you want early Developments to be softer and lighter while introducing the Threat to the PCs, and later Developments to really raise the stakes, push the goal forward, and seal the fate of the Threat itself.

You might sometimes start a later Development before resolving an earlier one. Follow what makes sense in the fiction and what makes for a better paced game in your evaluation at the moment. The example Threat below has far more Developments than needed, but when prepping you can pick and choose which are the most fitting and interesting.

By default, all Goals and Developments are what happens if the PCs don't intervene, and so can only be thwarted by the PCs. Other characters in the world can stall Threats, but if they can fully defeat them without help then those weren't real Threats to begin with.

### Changing and Ending a Threat

At some point the PCs will probably act against a Threat, such as stopping a Development or removing an Asset, thus making some of the aspects of a Threat stop making sense or being feasible. When this happens, you can either:

* **Rewrite it**. Find and create new Developments for the Threat to reach its goal. If the Threat is particularly tenacious you could even tweak the Goal itself. It should still be a problem for the world and the PCs, but the PCs have changed how those problems manifest. Maybe they're weaker or more desperate, maybe two small Threats join forces, or maybe the Threat's goals become much more ambitious.
* **End it**. If the Threat's goal is now impossible, unimportant, and/or uninteresting, then remove it as a Threat. *What happened to its Assets? Will any latent Secrets get revealed?*

### How Many Developments Should I Prep?

The number of Developments in a Threat can vary depending on how wide-reaching it is, how complicated its Goal is, and how long you intend them to be in the game. Here are some guidelines.

* *Local Threat*, simple adventure of the week, resolved in one session — 2-3 Developments
* *Regional Threat*, straightforward steps to its Goal, resolved in 2-5 sessions — 4-6 Developments
* *World Threat*, complex machinations to its Goal, resolved in 6+ sessions — 7-10 Developments

A Threat typically has a number of Assets and Reactions each loosely equal to half its number of Developments. So, a Threat with 7 Developments might have 4 Assets and 3 Reactions.

## Magic Items & Artifacts {#magic-items-&-artifacts}

Magic items are any objects that have exceptional or supernatural components to them. Sometimes they might not technically be magical, but with their effect might as well be. They often increase a user’s power, agency, and ability to affect the world around them.

Below are guidelines for how to use magic items in your game. You can change all of these to fit your world, tone, and goals for your game, but these are the default suggestions.

**Some magic items may tell you to mark a specific Condition**. If the Condition is already marked, lock it instead. If it’s already locked, lock another Condition of the GM’s choice.

### Encountering Magic Items

Magic items are almost never merely purchased. They are rare, often unique, and usually can only be found through adventures. Some campaigns might not have magic items at all.

When a PC acquires a magic item by [**Leveling Up**](#level-up), they choose from the [**Example Magic Items**](#heading=h.xzrlwpiylz0y) or create it themselves. When a magic item is found in the fictional world, the GM creates it.

Most magic items existed before they were found by the PCs, and likely affected the world and narrative then. Perhaps an enemy was using the item before they were felled, or the item was specifically sealed or hidden here for a purpose. Maybe the previous owner perished or lost it. The GM may often use them to shape or inform the adventure before the PCs acquire them.

### Identifying Magic Items

There are several ways to handle recognizing and learning to use magic items, depending on how you want to portray them and varying depending on the item itself.

* **Once you trigger its effects.** This is the default since players need to engage the item narratively before they see its mechanics. Even without definitively identifying it, PCs can intuit, research, [**Spout Lore**](#spout-lore-\(+questions\)), or ask around to get information about the magic item and how it might work.
* **When you [Make Camp](#make-camp)**, you automatically learn how it works, maybe you spend some time during your break experimenting and learning. This encourages Making Camp a bit more for groups that might want to avoid it, and can make the choice of when to do so more interesting.
* **When you first pick it up.** Maybe it imprints knowledge onto its holder, communicates directly, has written instructions, or it’s just really obvious how to use it.
* **When you get a specialist to reveal or unlock it**. Perhaps this magic item has a protective charm that needs to be broken before someone new can use it, or perhaps figuring magic items out is a very involved and dangerous process that has a dedicated profession in your world.

## Campaign Moves {#campaign-moves}

#### Enjoy Downtime {#enjoy-downtime}

| When you spend a few days resting in a safe place, you may do as many as you’d like of the following: *Carouse*. Spend 1 Treasure to clear a locked Condition or gain 1 Depth with another PC. *Resupply*. Spend 1 Treasure to refresh all uses on a PC’s non-magic items. *Shop*. Spend 1 Treasure to repair equipment, purchase items, or enlist services; you spend 1 Treasure per good/service. *Train*. Once per Downtime each PC can spend 1 Treasure to mark XP. |
| :---- |

In order to count as safe enough to trigger this Move, you usually need to rest in a well-guarded place without any imminent Threats. A makeshift camp in the wilderness isn't safe enough to really relax, no matter how hidden or well-defended you make it.

#### Forge a Bond {#forge-a-bond}

| When two PCs have 3 Depth with each other, clear both tracks and the Relationships, then choose one:  *Establish a new Bond*. Choose a Bond; write it on both character sheets in place of the Relationships. If you shared another Bond, erase it; both of you mark 1 XP for each Bond Advanced Move it had unlocked. *Reinforce an existing Bond*. Unlock a new Bond Advanced Move from it. *“How are you* really *feeling?”* Ask each other at least one question about your character’s thoughts, feelings, or motivations. If both answer truthfully, each marks 1 XP or clears 1 locked Condition. |
| :---- |

If one PC already has 3 Depth with another, they won't trigger this Move until that 3 Depth is matched in kind. If they would gain a 4th Depth with that PC, nothing happens. Remember that you can spend Depth to [**Aid a PC**](#aid-a-pc-\(n/a\)).

[**Example Bonds**](#example-bonds) can be found below.

#### Reveal Your Struggle {#reveal-your-struggle}

| When you [Make Camp](#make-camp) with a locked Condition, or when you [Level Up](#level-up), you may choose to confess a new, terrible truth about your PC’s Struggle, If you do, reveal a new Struggle or add a new detail to an already revealed one (and clear the locked Condition if any). | :---- |

[**Example Struggles**](#example-struggles) can be found below.

It's up to you what terrible truth you want to confess about your Struggle, but here are some examples:

* A powerful enemy knows all about your Struggle and how to exploit it
* An important NPC in the world shares or reflects the worst aspects of your Struggle
* Something about your Struggle was a lie; the truth is much worse
* There's another aspect of your Struggle that makes resolving it even more complicated
* You once did something terrible because of your Struggle
* You find evidence that your Struggle will only end in doom
* You're Struggle has put you, and maybe your allies, in much deeper trouble than anyone thought

## Bonds {#bonds}

Bonds represent the core unbreakable connection between two PCs. They aren’t always positive, and don’t negate disputes, but through Bonds the PCs work together and help each other overcome hardship and strife. By default a PC doesn’t start with any Bonds; a Bond must usually be forged during play.

While Relationships and their Depths are managed individually, Bonds and their Moves are mutually shared. When a Bond is first forged, both PCs immediately gain access to its Starting Move, and similarly Bond Advanced Moves become available to both PCs simultaneously.

* “You” refers to the singular PC triggering the bond’s Move
* “Your bondmate” refers to the other PC who isn’t triggering the Move, but is still part of this Bond with you. Certain bonds may instead use rival, student, teacher, or similar specific terms
* “Either of you” means you or your bondmate
* “Both of you” and similar refers to both bonded PCs

## Struggles {#struggles}

Struggles are powerful narrative aspects of a character that they grapple with over the course of play. All Struggles are, at their heart, a question about who the PC is, what matters to them, and what choices they are willing to make.

By default, each PC can only have one Struggle at a time. If you want to reveal a second Struggle, you must first resolve your current one.

**When a Struggle is revealed**, do each of the following:

* Read through the Struggle’s questions and decide which to answer now and which to explore in play
* Gain the Struggle’s benefits (+) and drawbacks (-) until resolved
* Clear a locked Condition, if you have one

**While you have a Struggle**, try to:

* Dramatically engage with both its benefit(s) and its drawback(s)
* Explore new details or emotions about it when appropriate, especially when someone [**Comforts or Supports**](#comfort-or-support) you
* Grapple with the hard choices or difficult circumstances it puts you in
* Ponder a terrible truth about it, which might be revealed in the future
* Resolve it only when narratively satisfying

**When another PC has a Struggle**, other Players should try to:

* Adapt your Relationship and/or Bond with them to their Struggle and how it affects the narrative
* Comfort, support, and sometimes challenge them regarding it
* Gasp in shock when a terrible truth is confessed
* Have your PC ask questions and express opinions about it

**The GM should reflect a PC Struggle in the world by**:

* Making it the focus of an obstacle, danger, or hard choice
* Portraying the benefits and drawbacks of it without holding back
* Presenting opportunities to resolve it when the time feels right

## Treasure & Wealth {#treasure-&-wealth}

*\[This section only matters for Campaigns. For a one-off session of* **Dungeon World 2***, you don't have to worry about Treasure or Wealth\]*

### Treasure

Most of the valuable things PCs will get their hands on come in the form of leftovers from a bygone age/civilization(s) (AKA “looting”). As such, these items vary in actual value when they’re sold to specialists and/or brokers. To avoid the confusion and hassle of having to assign these arbitrary values in the moment, we decided to abstract their value and simply refer to them in the rules as “Treasure”.

1 Treasure is then anything of potential value the PCs find and, unless the GM and/or the rest of the players consider that there’s an interesting Scene to be played when selling them, they’re available to be spent, anytime the PCs [**Enjoy Downtime**](#enjoy-downtime).

1 Treasure can come in many forms. The most usual way is as **a sack of coins**, but other alternatives include (but are not limited to):

* A piece of old non-magical equipment, such as **a battered breastplate**, **a functional spyglass**, or **an ornate sword**
* A small, mostly intact piece of ancient art, such as **a golden bracelet**, **an obsidian statuette**, or **a silver necklace**
* A substantial amount of common but valuable goods, such as **a bolt of linen**, **a cask of wine**, or **a few pounds of raw iron ore**
* An uncut, semi-precious stone, such as **a garnet**, **an amethyst**, or **a pearl**

Some items or collections of them can offer more value. In such cases, they’ll be described as 2-, 3-, or even 4-Treasure. Acquiring such large amounts of wealth is always a very risky proposition, as those tend to be well-guarded or have deadly security measures established long ago by their original owners.

### Wealth

Wealth is an abstract measure of the amount of money you have available to make purchases, your relative status in society, and which expenditures you need to worry about—and which ones you don’t. Wealth is determined by how much Treasure you’ve accumulated through adventuring, as in fantasy worlds no mundane/regular job would pay as well as risking your life to deal with the unusual and/or supernatural.

There are six tiers of Wealth in DW2 by default, from Broke to Aristocratic. You can always purchase any item of your Wealth (usually for 1 Treasure) and get access for free to any item corresponding to lower Wealth (within reason). The GM’s the ultimate arbiter as to whether something is available or not, so always consult with them first.

#### The Treasure Track

Every PC starts the game with a Treasure Track, which shows their accumulated Treasure and current Wealth. This track begins at Poor Wealth (all four Broke boxes already ticked). When a PC finds Treasure, they mark a number of boxes (going left to right) on their Treasure track equal to the Treasure's value (GM's call).  Once you mark the final slot on a specific Wealth tier, you advance to the next tier in the track.

Treasure is tracked individually, not as a group. If four PCs find something worth 3 Treasure, they will have to decide how to divide that up between them. What qualifies as treasure (and how much) also depends on a PC's Wealth. A small bag of silver coins might be chump change to a Rich PC (0 Treasure), or be a nice bonus for a Comfortable PC (1 Treasure), or cover the expenses of a Poor PC for weeks (2+ Treasure).

The Wealth tiers are as follows:

* **0 — Broke (4 slots)**. You have shelter from the elements, but you live in a desperate and often violent environment, rife with disease, hunger, and misfortune. You are beneath the notice of most people, and have few legal protections. You need to spend Wealth for basic expenses, and can't afford even simple comforts.
* **1 —** **Poor (6 slots)**. Simple food and lodgings, threadbare clothing, and unpredictable situations result in a sufficient but unpleasant experience. You benefit from some legal protections.
* **2 — Modest (8 slots)**. You’re out of the slums and can maintain your equipment. You don't go hungry or thirsty, and your lifestyle is clean, if simple.
* **3 —** **Comfortable (10 slots)**. You can afford nicer clothing and can easily maintain your equipment. You associate with merchants, skilled tradespeople, and military officers.
* **4 —** **Rich (12 slots)**. A life of luxury, though you might not have achieved the social status associated with it. You have respectable lodgings. You likely have a small staff of servants.
* **∞ —Aristocratic (No slots)**. A life of plenty and comfort. You move in circles populated by the most powerful people. You must also contend with the highest amounts of treachery & deceit. You don’t need to spend Treasure for any expense.

#### Making a Purchase

The GM confirms the cost of the item, taking into account which Wealth tier would treat it as a meaningful purchase, but not a trivial or impossible one.

* If the Cost is **below your Wealth**, your finances can painlessly absorb it, and you can buy it without clearing any boxes from your Treasure track

* If the Cost is **equal to your Wealth**, you'll still have to pay, but not a huge amount. Clear the rightmost box from your Treasure Track to buy it

* If the Cost is **above your Wealth**, you can't afford it, or those who sell it aren't willing to sell it to someone of your standing

## Items & Costs {#items-&-costs}

The lists below detail common useful things you might find or purchase while adventuring. These lists aren’t exhaustive, prices and details might vary depending on location, haggling, and similar factors. ##### **Selling Items:** Just like when buying items, sold items also relate to your Wealth. If an item's cost is equal to your Wealth, it sells for 0 Treasure (you're no merchant). If it's *above* your Wealth, it's worth 1 Treasure, no matter how much higher above your Wealth it is, unless you go out of your way to find a broker or make a deal

**Ignore Costs Below Wealth**: As your Wealth Standing rises, smaller costs are "absorbed" by your improved lifestyle, and can be ignored. If the cost of an item or service is equal to or less than your Wealth Standing, ignore it. This *does not* discount any costs that are above your Wealth level. *For example, Slick's new Modest standing lets him ignore costs of 1 or below, so he can buy medicine without spending Treasure.*

# Miniaturized Adventure: The Stolen Children {#miniaturized-adventure:-the-stolen-children}

*A Miniaturized Adventure Example* — Miniaturized Adventures are supposed to be condensed adventures for DW2 that fit in a single, double-sided sheet of paper. The idea is that they should contain everything you need in order to run the adventure, just in a compressed form. They’re great for convention play or to pull out and run out of the blue (if you need to).

Whenever checkboxes ☐ appear throughout the text that means that you, the GM, need to make a decision about what’s true for your version of the adventure.

**Content Warning: Implied violence against children**

*The children of Glen-kar are disappearing\! Rumours speak of alien woodland creatures seemingly made of briar & thorn, with almond-shaped eyes of a single hue, who’ve taken the youngsters hostage deep into Sleuth Wood, the feared forest nearby. The townspeople are desperate, and willing to offer everything they have in exchange for professional help. How far will you go to save the little ones, even for such a meager recompense?*

## GM Background {#gm-background}

* *In a time the Land forgot*… **Spinster of Shadows’ Threads (she/they)**, the Sovereign of Sleuth Wood, was happy.
* *Before your ancestors were born*… Spinster’s last partner passed away. She’s mourned them ever since.
* *In the past decade*… Spinster’s last child, **Cacophony of Silence (they/them)** died; they were killed ☐ deliberately or ☐ accidentally by some of Glen-kar’s inhabitants.
* *A few moons ago*… Spinster sent her subjects to kidnap Glen-kar’s mortal children to perform [**The Ruinreap Ritual**](#the-threat:-the-ruinreap-ritual).
* *Now*… The PCs arrive at Glen-kar and get involved, mostly ignorant of this tragedy’s origin.

## Setup {#setup}

* The people of Glen-kar possess no riches, so the reward they offer in exchange for help is barely a decent loot. Ask the PCs as a group: *Why did you decide to help the locals anyway?*
* One PC should write a positive Relationship with Spinster. Ask them: *What’s your Bond with Spinster? How did you acquire it?*
* Another PC should write a negative Relationship with Spinster. Ask them: *Why do you keep this grudge? Can it be resolved? If yes, how?*
* Ask a different PC: *What’s your special connection to Sleuth Wood? Why do you have it?*
* If there’s any PC who hasn’t answered a question yet, ask them: *Why do you suspect the adults of Glen-kar?*

## The Threat: The Ruinreap Ritual {#the-threat:-the-ruinreap-ritual}

*Spinster ordered their servants to bring her mortal children. She intends to use them as the main component of a dreadful ritual.*

**Goal** (Choose one) — ☐ Isolate Sleuth Wood ☐ Raise a forest army ☐ Unlock wondrous & terrible powers

### Assets

*Sabotaged ones impact Developments & Reactions*

* [ ] Fealty of her remaining Sylvan subjects
* [ ] Goodwill of the forest’s inhabitants
* [ ] Unswerving loyalty of The Hedge Guard

### Developments

*Happen in order; skip one or more if necessary*

* [ ] 1\. Collect materials—including willing innocent sacrifices
* [ ] 2\. Persuade her subjects of the wisdom of her decision
* [ ] 3\. Beseech the help of Sleuth Wood’s land itself
* [ ] 4\. Order Wildheart & the Hedge Guard to protect her while she performs the ritual
* [ ] 5\. Steal the mortal children’s ☐ capacity for love ☐ hopes & dreams ☐ lives (keep them forever young)
* [ ] 6\. Sacrifice her own lifeforce (Spinster’s) to complete the ritual; afterwards she’s deadly weakened until spring arrives

### Reactions

*Activate each as a GM Move*

* [ ] Alter the layout of Sleuth Wood to magically lose the PCs
* [ ] Bid the **Awakened Trees** to destroy Glen-kar
* [ ] Send **Wildheart** & the **Hedge Guard** to hunt down the PCs

### Scenes

*Can happen in any order, if at all. Fights not included*

* [ ] Meet with Kiela and discuss the terms of the PCs’ help
* [ ] Persuade Mossbones to intervene on behalf of the PCs with the forests’ inhabitants
* [ ] Discover Riri’s secret plans to get rid of Sleuth Wood
* [ ] Confront Kiela about Cacophony of Silence’s death
* [ ] Convince Wildheart & The Hedge Guard to stand down
* [ ] Find the stolen children; they’ve been treated very well
* [ ] Heal Spinster’s heart
* [ ] Navigate the dangerous maze of Sleuth Wood
* [ ] Receive Bonlyn’s help—and a nudge towards destroying Sleuth Wood

### Secrets

*Can be revealed whenever it makes sense, if at all*

* [ ] The new town mayor, Kiela, was directly responsible for Cacophony’s death. She knows the truth of the matter
* [ ] Spinster’s lost in her own sorrow & grief, and she doesn’t realize the harm she’s causing
* [ ] Riri was the main contributor to the reward. He did so mainly out of interest in professionals getting rid of the Sleuth’s ruler
* [ ] Mossbones has a great relationship with the Sleuth Wood’s inhabitants, and often goes deep into the haunted forest
* [ ] The stolen children have been convinced by Spinster that their sacrifice (and the following ritual) is for the best
